The Book of Job begins with the introduction of a man in the land of Uz, the titular character; Job. Uz, from the biblical clues appears to be a fertile plane in the Arabian peninsula, near where Edom would be. The time frame, many believe, is during the era of the patriarchs; Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

It would be good to note that, Abraham, or Abram, was a Chaldean, a nation who are mentioned here in this chapter located in what is modern Iraq. Where the seat of Babylonian power would be. And he was called out from the city of Ur there, to go unto Hebron by God. 

And, as mentioned throughout his story and his sons', there were people in this post flood era who feared the Lord, and offered sacrifices unto him. 

Of course we know about Melchizedek, King of Salem, and high priest, and the tenth of the spoils offered by Abram to Melchizedek. Many consider him to be a pre-incarnate appearance of Jesus. 

All this to say that the idea of the worship of God originating with Abraham, and only his seed after the flesh, is incorrect. There were many following the flood and the dispersion of nations out from Babel, that called upon the name of the Lord for salvation, and sought to offer the blood as a picture of their faith in Him, as we see Job does for himself and his family. 

One of the conceits of modern Zionism and dispensationalism holds that only the sons of Israel after the flesh were special, and that they invented monotheism. But throughout the Bible we find people who were not of that line, and not given to idols and false gods. It's important to understand that salvation has always been by grace alone through faith in the Lord, calling upon his name. And that salvation was always offered to all people. 

We will see in Exodus the mixed multitudes that followed Israel into the wilderness from Egypt. We will see high priests of other nations that fear the Lord. There will be people like Ruth and Rahab and others who have the faith. 

The distinction for Israel was that they were given the oracles of God. That they had the prophets and the word of God delivered directly to them, and given the law of the Lord that they may be perfect, complete, and a light unto the Gentiles. The other nations. Calling all men to repentance. 

But we see the greatest man on Earth at this moment, according to God, who feared Him, eschewing evil, offering sacrifices to sanctify himself and his family. Without the law. Without the temple. Without a priesthood. Without the oracles of God, knowing by the faith of his fathers what was expected.

Now God knows who Satan is and what he does and His pointing out Job is deliberate, and in so many ways this is done for our admonition, upon whom the end of the world has come. 

If a man as upright and perfect in his ways can be sorely tempted by the persecution of Satan against him, If the hedge of protection may be lifted, to show the glory of God for such a man as job, how can we expect the small tragedies that befall us as believers to be any less to His glory? 

If the Book of Job was one chapter, the lesson is present and clear. But as we'll see, there is so many more lessons to learn than simple perseverance in faith. 

Satan of course credits the hedge of protection God has placed around Job and his family and the blessings of the holdings that he's acquired which are gifts of God, as the only reason Job's faith is so strong. He says to God that if He put forth His hand now and touched all that he had, Job would curse Him to his face.

Now the Lord says to Satan, *behold all that he hath is in thy power* but bars him from doing harm to Job physically.

Before the second messenger can finish, a third arrives and says the Chaldeans made out three bands and fell upon the camels and slaying the servants. Of course this is a raiding party from the north, striking simultaneously with those from the south.

We see here that worship is defined as falling to your knees or bowing down. Job in absolute sorrow tears his clothes, shaves his head for the dead, and lays face first down upon the ground.

*Naked came I out of my mother's womb, and naked shall I return thither: the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord.*

And despite all of this tragedy, despite all of the cause Satan gave him to sin, Job sinned not. And he didn't blame God, but rather blessed His name.

Thematically, as prose, this setup creates tension in the reader. I'll probably say this multiple times throughout this study, but the Book of Job is the finest example of literature ever written. 

While its exact date of composition isn't known, it likely found its final form during the Babylonian captivity. 

Shortly thereafter, in the Hellenistic world, the Greek drama became a literary style. Acted out by people, to tell a story either from a monologue or a soliloquy or a troupe of performers. 

Because we are gifted with the King James version, and it's eloquent prose in what many would say is Shakespearean English, it's easy to picture the Book of Job as a play. And it is possible to perform, and many have. And it is greater than all of the plays the Bard penned. 

Isn't it interesting that it was put to pen under the inspiration of God, separate from and before the Greek tragedies and comedies that the world now considers classic literature?

Job 4 will begin a dialogue between Job and the three friends who came to him to mourn. 

The first to reply to Job's plaintive cry, cursing the day he was born, is Eliphaz the Temanite, of Teman, a city in Edom. (**Genesis 36:11, 15; Jeremiah 49:7, 20; Ezekiel 25:13**). His initial argument is made in chapters 4 and 5, and offers a view of "retribution theology," the idea of karma in God's doings. That sin is the cause of suffering, and that you get what you deserve. 

Job’s friends defend retribution theology rigorously, and it is difficult to disagree with many of the points made, from a human perspective. Much of it sounds right in our minds. So, before we dive deeper into the text, we should keep in mind the end of the matter, where (spoilers), God addresses the men and what they say. 

**Job 42:7-9**
*And it was so, that after the Lord had spoken these words unto Job, the Lord said to Eliphaz the Temanite, My wrath is kindled against thee, and against thy two friends: for ye have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job hath. Therefore take unto you now seven bullocks and seven rams, and go to my servant Job, and offer up for yourselves a burnt offering; and my servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept: lest I deal with you after your folly, in that ye have not spoken of me the thing which is right, like my servant Job. So Eliphaz the Temanite and Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went, and did according as the Lord commanded them: the Lord also accepted Job.*

The friends of Job, (and later, the young accuser Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite), are given opportunity to make multiple philosophical and theological points regarding the nature of God, His justice and their natural suspicion of those who suffer as being sinners, and worse, that those that suffer worse than they do must be greater sinners than they are. 

This line of thought is just as common today as it was then, and is human nature. But not God's. And while there is general wisdom to be found in their arguments, the application they choose to make is wrong. 

As we go, it is good to keep this in mind, especially when we read something that seems perfectly reasonable to us, and may reflect our own views that we may have held against others in our life. It should cause self-reflection.

So, to recap: Job, devastated by the loss of his children, wealth and health, and wishing he was never born, while yet not sinning against God or cursing Him, sits in ash, scraping his sore-covered flesh, in agony. 

He had been blessed by these three well meaning friends, who traveled afar, and patiently, in love, sat mourning in silence for a week. But with the silence broken by Job's lamentation, Eliphaz the Temanite now speaks up. 

He starts by asking that, if they attempt to talk with him, will it offend Job? And then immediately, before Job can answer, Eliphaz gives himself permission by saying "Who can withhold themselves from speaking?"

We have all likely, at one time or another been on the receiving or delivering end of this. "I know you're going through a lot, so I don't want to offend you, but I can't help myself..."

As well intentioned as this sentiment can be, what follows it rarely is a help to the person hearing it. Ultimately, the motivation behind it is self-centered and doesn't come from sympathy for the aggrieved, but out of an impatient empathy, where the friend sees it from their detached view, and thinks their opinions and musing can fix what's broken, or even just make the person stop complaining. 

Many times, after having given our two-cents, we can feel comfortable washing our hands of any obligation to be bothered by a friend's burdens. "Well, if they'd just listen to reason...Oh well, I tried."

Better to have remained silent than to do this.

Eliphaz the Temanite barrels along right into it. He begins by pointing out the example Lot has been for many, that his faith and council have helped those in troubled times. Strengthening the weak. But...it is never good to follow a compliment with a "but," but now that it has come upon Job, he faints and is troubled. 

Rather than encouraging Job to embrace the same strength and hope that he gave others, Eliphaz uses this good quality of his friend as an indictment. As an accusation of weakness. 

I would doubt that any of the people Job had encouraged had gone through anything even remotely touching the tribulation that he found himself in. But even so, rather than repaying the favor and encouraging Job, Eliphaz makes an accusation out of it. 

*Is not this thy fear, thy confidence, thy hope, and the uprightness of thy ways?*

And when Job prayed for his friends, the Lord turned the captivity of Job, the allowances of the devil to control his fate, and restored Job twice as much as he had. 

In all these things Job never spoke improperly, but in ignorance demanded of God. But it was Job's forgiveness of others who had wronged him that led God to restore him, and bless him with more than he had.

Job reached peace with God just by his understanding and faith. But it was forgiveness of others that restored him. This is such an important point to remember. 

Job first receives the comforting and support, from family and friends, that he needed. And their financial support to him that was without, allowed Job to restore his wealth. The love and respect Job had shown others paid off, as they loved on him with emotional support and alms. Gold and riches he used to make his flocks great again. 

And God blessed him with 10 children, seven sons and 3 daughters. And Jobs delight in his daughters, naming them for peace, and fragrant spice and beauty, led him to make them co-heirs as their brothers.

The Lord gave Job a long life, to see four generations come from these children. And so Job died, being old and full of days. Quite a ways away from his lamenting his own birth. 

Our struggles and temptations may never be as horrific and total as Job's test. We may seek to know why from the Lord. We may be tempted to blame Him, to curse God and die, as Job's wife said. Others may use the occasion to imply we deserve it. 

But we should see the opportunity to better know God. To grow our faith, and our understanding of Him. To know that here, or in the resurrection, we shall overcome. 

The Lord will deliver us, He has turned the captivity, the bondage of sin we are all under, to liberty in Christ. We have the Comforter sent by Him. Our later end will always be greater than our former, because we are all heirs in Jesus.

A reflection on **Job** and his later end being better than his first, and the salvation of his (and our) Redeemer:

Like Job, we are born of woman and blessed by God to an abundant life, no matter our station, having life. When temptation comes, and the law enters, and sin slays us, the Accuser, Satan, does his worst to us (tempered by God's mercy). 

We have trials, and tribulations. But, entering into grace by faith, we have the hope and promise of God in Christ that our end will be better than our beginning. That at the adoption, when we put on immortality and shed corruption, when we are redeemed, our end will be glorious, as we will be like Him. And like Lot's children, male or female, we are made joint heirs with Christ.
