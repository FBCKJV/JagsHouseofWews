John hits the ground running. No human genealogies. Not the human birth of Christ, but the divine godhead of Jesus. 

The Word is God's promise. The Word is the written testimony of God to man. The Word is who made all things and gave life, to creation and man. He was the light. He is God, and always was.

John begins his testimonial of the good news letting the reader know beyond a shadow of a doubt that Jesus is God, and was with God in the beginning.

Chapter 13 is a fairly straightforward account. Jesus' last supper before Passover, where He washes the apostle's feet, in an act to teach them of the humility and love for one another that will exemplify to others the love the saved have in Christ. 

Note, in the Gospel of John, he is who "Jesus loved," who was laying on His bosom. 

His hour drawing near, he reveals the son of perdition, Judas, that it is he who will betray him. Satan himself enters Judas the unbelieving, sending him on his way to the Pharisees after Jesus shows him He knows.

Jesus again explains His mission, so that afterwards they may know He was who He said. 

Peter is the foil twice in the chapter, first being overly zealous to show Jesus how much he loves Him, tries to stop Christ from washing his feet. Once corrected, Peter, dumb as his nickname, a stone, says "well then, wash my whole body!" 

Later Peter again in his zeal proclaims his devotion, only to again be corrected by Jesus, and that he will actually deny Christ 3 times. 

Sometimes, like Peter we get so anxious to please God that we get our own ideas of what that means and what we must do. Instead of slowing down and listening to what God will have us do. And often, we find correction comes in His word, in prayer and when tempted for what we swear, to show it as the folly it is. Peter did great things for the Lord when he stopped to listen.

Another note on this chapter, John's account of the last supper. As we have said throughout the Gospel according to John, this is written specifically to the unsaved so that they may understand and come to salvation by faith. So it is important to recognize that there is no account of the breaking of bread and blessing of wine, which were symbols used to explain Jesus' coming trials and death in the other Gospel accounts. 

If a ceremony of eating bread and drinking wine were necessary for salvation, it would be mentioned here. Of course if it were mentioned, it would contradict the entire message of God's grace giving us eternal life through belief on His Son found in every chapter.

The final chapter in the Gospel of John is a kind of postscript to John's account of his time with Jesus during His earthly ministry and after His resurrection, a personal recounting of one of the appearances of the risen Christ, and a redemption arc for the apostle Peter.

Now, during this time, after having been shown their faith wasn't in vain, that Jesus is the Christ and the Son of God, but prior to His final ascension on Pentecost, it seems that some of the disciples, Simon Peter chief among them, grew restless absent their Lord. Jesus appeared unto them but wasn't the ever present fixture leading them He had been for the past 3 years. Yes they were saved, yes they knew they had salvation, and yes they knew God's word was true, and yet when left to their own devices, Peter and the others returned to their old ways. Gone a fishing.

This is something many if not most newer Christians experience, or even young Christians brought up in the faith. The rush of joy and the zeal for our Lord gives way to our habits and old life, or, without direction fades to mundane tasks of this world instead of living for the God who bought them. 

As David, a man after God's own heart wrote: *Restore unto me the joy of Thy salvation; and uphold me with Thy free spirit.* It happens to the best of us. That level of zeal is difficult to maintain at times. 

Peter, one of the greatest Christians, who did so much for the Lord experienced this, and his leadership role lead him to take others along with him, back to the everyday life they'd left behind following Jesus. Not backslidden in sin necessarily, but back to the grind of the world. Carnal but not sinful. More aimless than lost. And here Jesus intervenes.

After an unsuccessful night of fishing, Jesus appears on the shore and in a familiar manner to earlier stories in the Gospels, He directs them to cast their empty nets another way, and they are amazed at the fill of the catch. 

I think this is both real and a picture of the ministry to come in the great commission and the disciples being "fishers of men." That where there's sparsity of "fish" among the Jews, Jesus is showing them that casting a wider net out in a different direction, to the Gentiles, that the nets will be overflowing and yet not burst. As many fish as they can haul in. 

Jesus dines with Peter, John, Thomas and the others, feeding them bread and fish He provides, over the fire He built. A picture of things.

The risen Christ is a bodily resurrection, Jesus eats. He's not a ghost or spirit, but risen in the flesh. 

After they eat, Jesus tempts Peter three times with the question "*Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me?"* And Peter, three times, swears that he does, and that Jesus knows that he does. Just as Peter had denied Jesus thrice before the cock crowed just weeks earlier, Jesus has given him a chance to redeem himself, and direction for his life if he truly loved Jesus. "Feed my sheep."

This is the direction given to all saved believers, to be part of that royal priesthood. To be His disciples. To share the Gospel and win the souls of those lost sheep. To be fishers of men. It's a personal and touching form of the great commission. 

Jesus indicates to Peter where his discipleship shall lead, and John as narrator explains to the reader that it's to a martyrs death that glorified God if Peter follows Him. If he feeds His sheep.

With that understanding, Jesus says "Follow me," and Peter does, with John coming along. 

Here we see another instance of Peters personality, why Jesus named him a stone. Peter, processing the death he should die for his following Jesus and feeding His sheep turns and points to John and says basically, "what about him?" It's classic Simon Peter. 

Jesus' reply, "*If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee? follow thou me."* is a gentle rebuke and reminder that we aren't to worry so much about how other saved Christians walk their Christian life, but rather concentrate on our own. If one does nothing, that's not excuse for ourselves.
