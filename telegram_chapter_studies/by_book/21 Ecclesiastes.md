Following **Proverbs,** the collected wise sayings and instructions of Solomon, the wisest man in history, we have **Ecclesiastes**, the preaching of the King that collects his life observations that fortified that wisdom. 

Solomon identifies himself as the author, and the thesis of his sermon is given in the 2nd verse. 

*Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity.*

I picture Solomon preaching this to the court, or perhaps the congregation from his eponymous porch, located at the Temple he had built. A crowd convicted by his words, and the Spirit behind them. Shouts of "Amen!" throughout. 

King Solomon's reign is traditionally dated to the 10th century BC, 4-500 years before Greek and ancient Chinese philosophy is recorded, and this extended sermon on the vanity of life, (and its ultimate purpose), is clearer, and more wise than all of the vain philosophy of men put together. 

The preacher asks, *What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?*

Men die. Men are born. The earth abides. The sun rises and sets. The wind comes and goes, from the North and the South, changing with the seasons. 

Everything runs in these cycles, he says. 

The rivers run to the sea, yet the sea is never filled. The water, returning to the place where the rivers come from. 

(This understanding of the water cycle predates scientific explanation of it by nearly 2,500 years. The sharpest Greek minds thought there were springs underground, carrying the water back to the source.)

All is vanity. All things are full of labor, work is never done. The eye doesn't stop seeing, ear hearing. The senses never are filled. 

*The thing that hath been, it is that which shall be; and that which is done is that which shall be done:* Nothing ever happens. All things have already been, and things that will be have already been. A truth that even the secular world will cite; T*here is no new thing under the sun*.

Every "new" thing a man discovers was old, and done before. We forget, and rediscover. Things we discovered will be forgotten. The cycle will continue. 

And so, Solomon, the Preacher, as King of Israel sought out the wisdom of all things done under Heaven. The *sore travail* God has given to men in this life. Having established there is nothing new under the sun, nor will there ever be, and having observed all these things, he came to a conclusion; 

...*all is vanity and vexation of spirit. That which is crooked cannot be made straight: and that which is wanting cannot be numbered*. The travails of life are fed by this truth. And so, all is vanity. 

Solomon says, he gave his full heart to experience all things, to feed the wisdom God gave him, to increase his knowledge more than any man before him. 

*And I gave my heart to know wisdom, and to know madness and folly: I perceived that this also is vexation of spirit.*

He says that even this quest to know all things is vanity, since all things that can be known are part of this endless cycle, that ran before he ever came, and continues now, long after he departed the earth. And this has brought him much grief; *he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow.*

Now, we are called to pursue wisdom, to gain understanding, to know the ways of the Lord and to follow His paths, rather than following after folly. 

But the accumulation of much knowledge of the things of this world, in the eyes of finite man, the entropy and atrophy of a fallen world, and endless labor, only to die; increases our sorrow. 

The seeming futility of it all, reinforced by observation. A feeling even the simple who has noticed life in their limited experience can relate to. The opening of the sermon as a depressing hook that captures that feeling of vexation, which Solomon will use to bring us to the truth.

In **Ecclesiastes 1**, Solomon established his underlying idea, that all of life is vain, meaningless, full of travails, trials and hardships, that every life is full of tribulation, a cycle of never-ending labor. Now, the preacher begins to expound. 

He starts chapter 2 by explaining how he, to test all things, gave in to the pleasures of the heart; mirth, laughter. Wine and merrymaking. Partying, as we would say today. And he found it wanting. 

He continued in wisdom, while allowing himself folly, testing all things to see what was good that man should do. A scientific approach that rationalizes sin in the name of gained knowledge. Solomon, a King gifted with supernatural wisdom sought pleasures for a season, to prove wisdom. Yet, this approach to life is honestly all too typical for man. 

We sow our wild oats. We all have our time of life where we question the wisdom of our parents, the law and ways of God, the judgment of our conscience, to see what good can be found in the ways of the world. And, for most of us, we grow out of it. God's chastening and the reality of the wages of sin, bringing us back from folly, to see these things as vain. Ineffectual and worthless.

Wisdom teaches better, but folly is doing what you know you shouldn't, and expecting a better result than doing the right thing. Reality is a harsh tutor, and when it comes to the party lifestyle, you don't have to be the wisest man ever to live to see it is folly. 

Yet many fools continue to chase after mirth, drunken merrymaking, long after it has cost them everything and brutally affected the lives of those around them.

<!-- post 17464 · 2026-05-03 06:49 · study -->

Solomon then talks of the great works he undertook, to redeem the time in more constructive ways. Building houses, planting vineyards, gardens and orchards and every kind of budding fruit trees. He made pools of water to irrigate his great gardens. 

He had many servants, even generational, born in his house, all to staff his households and agricultural enterprises. He owned much cattle, great and small, livestock more than any other before him. 

The accumulation of wealth, the peculiar treasures of kings, unique offerings and crafts made of precious stones. He gathered unto himself silver and gold in abundance. 

The description of Solomon's possessions, the magnitude of his wealth, seem at first blush to be beyond our ability to understand or relate to. But, within these pursuits we should find familiarity. 

We all seek to build out our home. We plant our gardens, both literally and metaphorically, utilizing what we have to build something more. We all have projects and plans we devote time and resources to. We may lack servants and palaces and peculiar riches, but we all strive to have more. 

Solomon used his position to have multiple singers, male and female, musicians and the delight of music to entertain him. Again, we may see this and think, "he is a king, of course he can command this, I can't relate." But today, even the most poor among us has instant access to the greatest singers and musicians of the past century, to listen to the delights of the sons of men greater than even Solomon at his height could ever hear. 

The Preacher says *So I was great, and increased more than all that were before me in Jerusalem: also my wisdom remained with me.*

And in that wisdom, these pursuits, as cyclical and vain as they were, did produce joy. He says he *withheld not my heart from any joy; for my heart rejoiced in all my labour: and this was my portion of all my labour.* The brief moments of joy all of this brought him, he considered his reward. 

But, beyond the temporary rejoicing, he perceived the underlying problem; death. 

Looking at all he worked for, all he gained, and the fruit of that labor, and he sees the same end as the mirth and laughter. 

...*behold, all was vanity and vexation of spirit, and there was no profit under the sun.*

All the things that man could do, under the Sun, had been done before. Even by a King who could do more, and more abundantly than any other man, and it was all in vain, without lasting meaning. Even his son, Rehoboam would be unable to achieve anything new; Solomon had exhausted the most extreme versions of all that existed and was known to man under the Sun. But his wisdom remained;

*Then I saw that wisdom excelleth folly, as far as light excelleth darkness. The wise man's eyes are in his head; but the fool walketh in darkness: and I myself perceived also that one event happeneth to them all.* Death.

The end comes for the fool and the wise alike. And the depressin*g* notion came to him, thinking, *As it happeneth to the fool, so it happeneth even to me; and why was I then more wise? Then I said in my heart, that this also is vanity.*

What good is wisdom, if like the pleasures and labors of life, it all ends in vanity? What good is the pursuit of discernment and knowledge, to know the ways of the world and gather wisdom from experiencing all that life has to offer, if in the end it dies with you, as the folly of a fool dies with him?

If this is all that life has, if the meaning of it is to toil, travail, and then die, then all is vanity and vexation of spirit. Solomon says he hated the vanity of life, hated all his worthless labor, which in the end would be left to an heir who may or may not value it, who may or may not be wise, or a fool, and squander whatever inheritance there may be. The projects and planning and pleasures, and treasure and toil, all are vanity.

The works of wisdom and in knowledge and equity, fair dealings and not being a respecter of persons, the wisdom we saw again and again in **Proverbs**, is the same portion as those that did not pursue wisdom. *This also is vanity and a great evil.*

If our labor is in vain, and the days full of sorrows, and our works and plans lead to sleepless nights and grief, leaving behind little lasting legacy, what is the point? 

*There is nothing better for a man, than that he should eat and drink, and that he should make his soul enjoy good in his labour.* 

This is a repeated idea throughout Ecclesiastes, and in Scripture; if all is vanity, and nothing matters, better to eat, drink and be merry. 

**Isaiah 22:13**
 *And behold joy and gladness, slaying oxen, and killing sheep, eating flesh, and drinking wine: let us eat and drink; for to morrow we shall die.*

As Jesus says of the rich man, who like Solomon had stored up riches; 

Luke 12:16-19
*And he spake a parable unto them, saying, The ground of a certain rich man brought forth plentifully: And he thought within himself, saying, What shall I do, because I have no room where to bestow my fruits? If the only reward that man may have for all our toil in this life, for worldly things, is the brief joy that comes from resting in the fruit of our labor, then perhaps, Solomon reasons, this is something to offset the vexation, the depression that comes from seeing all things as vanity. The wisdom of Solomon gives us the first glimpse of hope and purpose;

*This also I saw, that it was from the hand of God.*

The gift of those moments where we enjoy the good of our labors, is from God. *For God giveth to a man that is good in his sight wisdom, and knowledge, and joy*. The virtue of wisdom and walking in the ways of the Lord is superior to the ways of the fool, the sinner, who has greater travails and endless labor as their reward, which is again vain and vexing. 

At the first mention of God in this sermon, we can start to see a glimpse into the everlasting. To see life's meaning and purpose. 

If the fool labors more, heaping up and gathering what the wise will ultimately gain, to be given to him that is good before God (as echoed in **Proverbs 13:22** *A good man leaveth an inheritance to his children's children: and the wealth of the sinner is laid up for the just.*) and not enjoying the God given gifts of their labor, then all their efforts are vain. 

The Preacher is building a case, and the answer to vanity and vexation of spirit, and we see here the first hint of what the answer is, by the grace of God towards the just.

*To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven*.

This biblical truth has influenced all of mankind. Even those that reject the word of God accept this philosophy of time and discernment as wisdom. 

Solomon the Preacher has been making the case of futility in life. That the cyclical nature of things, the endless travails, trials and tribulation man goes through, only to die and be forgotten, regardless of how he lives, is vanity and vexation of spirit.

In **Ecclesiastes 2**, he drew a distinction, the first glimmer of hope, by the acknowledgement that the fruit of our labors, the food and drink God blesses man with, is good. That the treasure the wicked heap up for themselves is destined for the righteous. 

Here, in chapter 3, Solomon continues the sermon, opening with the understanding that there are appropriate times for everything, seasons of life; birth and death. Sowing and reaping. Killing and healing. Destruction and construction. 

Life has moments of joy and despair. Hatred and forgiveness. Economic ups and downs. Purging and gathering. War and peace. 

To everything, in its season, to every purpose, in every life, these truths apply. And the wisdom of knowing what season you're in, or observing their change, and doing the appropriate thing, is discernment. 

Having the understanding that in the midst of triumph, there are seeds of destruction, and in mourning, joy, is a gift of God. 

But, again, life is a cycle. These things are necessary, but they too are vanity.

*What profit hath he that worketh in that wherein he laboureth?* What's the point of all this labor and toil? The empty exercise of taking land that turns back again to wilderness, the things built up and eventually torn down. The embrace that ends in rejection.

**Ecclesiastes 7** is the true beginning of the culmination of the Preacher's sermon, where the wisdom gained by observation of all things under the Sun, and the ways of man and God, leads Solomon to many conclusions regarding life.
