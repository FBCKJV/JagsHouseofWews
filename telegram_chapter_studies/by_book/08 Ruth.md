**Ruth** chapter 1, a tale of a progenitor of Christ. 

**Matthew 1:1-17**
*The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham. Abraham begat Isaac; and Isaac begat Jacob; and Jacob begat Judas and his brethren; And Judas begat Phares and Zara of Thamar; and Phares begat Esrom; and Esrom begat Aram; And Aram begat Aminadab; and Aminadab begat Naasson; and Naasson begat Salmon; And Salmon begat Booz of Rachab; and Booz begat Obed of Ruth; and Obed begat Jesse*...

It is interesting that the grandmother and great-grandmother of David were a Moabite and a Canaanite harlot of Jericho. That Jesus' human lineage is not one of ethnic purity. Jacob married Leah, a woman of Mesopotamia, and sired Judah. Abraham was a Chaldean. The genetic claim of Jews, so-called, to the kingdom comes through intermarrying with other nations and people. 

Also worth pointing out they are the only two women mentioned by name in the genealogy given by Matthew.

<!-- post 13851 · 2024-04-07 07:18 · study -->

In the narrative, we see this is set during the period of Judges, where as we know, men did that which was right in their own eyes. And so we find a man of Judah, Elimelech and his wife, Naomi, deciding to leave the promised land during a famine, and seek their fortune sojourning for a season in Moab. They are there long enough for their sons Mahlon and Chilion to wed Moabite woman, Orpah and Ruth. 

And in the course of time, Elimelech and his sons die in Moab, and Naomi, hearing the famine had ended in Israel, seeks to return to her people. The decision to leave the promised land seeking bread turned out tragically for her. Her husband doing what was right in his own eyes left her bitter and feeling cursed by God for their bad choices. 

So, Naomi sets out to return to Judah, and tells her tearful daughters-in-law they are free to return to their people and their gods. Orpah does. Ruth refuses, vowing to return with Naomi, to make Israel her people, and the Lord God her God. Till death do they part. 

So, returning to Bethlehem and created by those who recognize Naomi, she tells them to call her Mara, meaning "bitter," because she says, *The Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me.*

Continuing the story of **Ruth**, we see her meeting the kin of her father-in-law Elimelech, Boaz. A man of Judah, of some wealth. Owning fields outside of Bethlehem with servants reaping the barley harvest, and maidens attending the fields. 

As Ruth vowed before the Lord, *for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God*. Her responsibility is to care for her mother-in-law, as her own mother. And being destitute in their widowhood, Ruth goes out to gather the gleaning for herself and Naomi. 

**Leviticus 23:22**
*And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not make clean riddance of the corners of thy field when thou reapest, neither shalt thou gather any gleaning of thy harvest: thou shalt leave them unto the poor, and to the stranger: I am the LORD your God.*

Gleaning is God's method of feeding the poor and the stranger. That they work to eat from the leftovers in the field after the harvest. A kindness, and effective means of maintaining the welfare of the people, and a picture of the blessings that fall unto the stranger by being around a group of godly believers, who in their faith are blessed beyond their needs. And in partaking of the blessings, may themselves come to believe. 

Ruth already has, it seems. Making the God of Israel her God. 

Ruth catches the eye of Boaz, who has come from the city to see the harvest. The servants tell him of her diligence in gleaning, her hard work, and her service to Naomi is known to him. 

Boaz tells her to stay with his maidens and servants, to glean no other field. To take freely the water his servants had drawn. That she will be safe with them. He even feeds her supper, until she is fully sufficed.

 *Then she fell on her face, and bowed herself to the ground, and said unto him, Why have I found grace in thine eyes, that thou shouldest take knowledge of me, seeing I am a stranger?*

It reminds me of **Psalm 8** the psalmist talking to God; *What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him?*

Ruth found grace in the eyes of the lord of the harvest. The salvation imagery is so powerful here. 

As Boaz says to Ruth; *The Lord recompense thy work, and a full reward be given thee of the Lord God of Israel, under whose wings thou art come to trust.* Her trust is in the Lord, and so her works receive recompense.

Believers are rewarded for diligently seeking the Lord. For putting their trust in Him, their works receive recompense. 

**Hebrews 11:6**
*But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him.*

*Then she said, Let me find favour in thy sight, my lord; for that thou hast comforted me, and for that thou hast spoken friendly unto thine handmaid, though I be not like unto one of thine handmaidens.*

The salvation of God by faith in Him was always available to all men. Even a Moabite woman. It was never exclusive to "the Jews." Israel was to be a light to the world to bring them to repentance. 

Boaz is a Godly man, following the law. He recognized Ruth by her chaste conversation, her behavior. And by extending his grace on her, that she may live off his bounty, she is thankful. 

So she cleaned until even, and brought home to Naomi a bushel of barley flour that she had ground out of her gleaning, telling Naomi it was of Boaz. 

*And Naomi said unto her daughter in law, Blessed be he of the Lord, who hath not left off his kindness to the living and to the dead.*

The bitter "Mara" seeing that God is blessing her. That His kindness to His children is eternal. To the living and the dead in Him. 

And so, Ruth kept fast to the maidens of Boaz until the end of the wheat harvest.

Boaz tells him there is a field of Elimelech's that Naomi seeks to sell, and says to the man, would you redeem this that it stays with the inheritance, and the man gladly accepts. He would redeem the land that it stay with their kin.

The laws of the right of redemption, and the process of securing a means of redeeming a property to keep it as part of the inheritance of the extended family, and more importantly, the redemption of the childless widow, to keep the dead man's name, to raise the dead to its inheritance, are a like picture of Christ. 

In this case, you see the nearer kinsman offered the right to redeem the parcel of land, and his willingness to do so, but his inability to redeem the person, Ruth, and raise the name of the dead. This is a picture of the Law itself.

By the Law the people could maintain their land, to keep the inheritance within the family. But the Law is unable to redeem the person, and raise their name from the dead unto their inheritance. 

Boaz, picturing Christ Jesus, desires to, and is able, to do both. 

And Ruth, a figure of the believer who has called upon the Lord to save her, bearing fruit and building up Israel, the Israel of God, that are other believers. 

It was a shame for the nearest kin to refuse redemption. The widow would remove the shoe of the man who rejected the right, and spit in his face before the people. Here, because of the amicable arrangement and willingness of Boaz, the man simply removes his shoe, to show he is less worthy than the son of Judah who has fufiled the role. 

Similarly, John the Baptist, when speaking of the Messiah, said of his cousin, Jesus;

**John 1:26-27**
*John answered them, saying, I baptize with water: but there standeth one among you, whom ye know not;*
*he it is, who coming after me is preferred before me, whose shoe's latchet I am not worthy to unloose.*

Just as water baptism had no power to regenerate the dead - to rise them up that their name not be blotted out of Israel, unto their inheritance, redeemed - but Christ could. John says he is not worthy to even loosen the laces of Jesus' shoe. 

In a like manner the law was incapable of redemption, but by Jesus we are bought. We are paid for with a price. We are redeemed.
