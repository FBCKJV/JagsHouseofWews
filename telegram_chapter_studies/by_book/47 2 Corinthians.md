Paul writes his second epistle to the church at Corinth, approximately a year following the first. As we know from Acts, his plan to go to them was derailed, slightly and instead, elders of the church went to him as he traveled, and so he writes with some apology in his greeting. That the troubles he came to in his travels that prevented him from going to them, were actually blessings. 

He acknowledges the truth of the Gospel is in them, as it is in him, and that of the hope of eternal life we also receive the comfort of God in the Holy Spirit. 

The message, that in our tribulations and troubles, we have comfort, given by God's grace, and in that comfort, the ability to give the same comfort with grace an understanding to others in need. Thus, his tribulation and that which the churches experience are a gift, that gives us consolation and salvation from trouble, that we may provide the same to others. 

He reminds them that if our faith in God is to the hope of the resurrection and eternal life, then there's nothing we can't place on God to deliver for us. 

This is the goal of a Christian life, to see not only riches and good fortune as blessings, but troubles and tribulations even unto death, as giving glory to God.

The gift of prayer for others is a gift that is repaid with thanks in prayer, towards God, "Who hath also sealed us, and given the earnest of the Spirit in our hearts." 

We are sealed unto the day of judgment, and the ernest, or the down payment on the promise of eternal life, is the Spirit living within our hearts. And so with this knowledge, the testimony of our faith living within us, we can trust in God. His promises are 'yea, yea.'

It's a beautiful introduction and sober reminder that in God, all things are possible and we needn't be troubled by anything in this world, because in Christ we have already overcome it.

<!-- post 3999 · 2022-07-28 08:44 · study -->

The final line in chapter 1, regarding the apostle and the evangelists who have ministered to the churches throughout the region, and specifically to Corinth not being in dominion over the believers there, but helpers, because they are established and stand by their own faith as individuals. Their churches are independent as well. They may receive reproach or guiding, but it's not to dictate over them who are equal in the faith, members of the same body with Christ at its head.

Chapter 2 continues Paul's introduction, speaking now as to why he hadn't come to them, *with a rod,* as promised in his first epistle to them, saying he decided in part to not come to them "in heaviness" to grieve them. 

As we saw in 1 Corinthians, Paul had laid into them pretty hard, especially in chapter 5 regarding one member and his wicked fornication. 

Paul says to them that he considers them his sons in Christ, having asked them to follow him as a father who brought them to the gospel, and like a father, scourging those he loves, Paul says his chastisement was necessary but he doesn't want them to mistake it for anything other than his love of them. 

He says in verse 5 that it wasn't personal. He's not doing this out of hurt feelings.

And likewise, as he's showing kindness to those he'd chastised, he says to them that they should be forgiving to those in the church that they punished as he'd called for, and they having repented, be welcomed back and comforted that they not fall back into sin.

We are never to tolerate or enable other Christians sins, especially those that lead to church discipline, but when a Christian makes a sincere turn around, we are to be gracious like God is with us, daily. Forgiveness of trespasses. 

Paul says if they've forgiven some their sin and brought them back from being backslidden, it's good enough for him, because it is good enough for Christ. 

This may be specifically the man who was sleeping with his step-mother, but it reads as a more general item. We should be happy to forgive and forget, just as eagerly as we are to condemn and rebuke. 

As a parent treats their child, which is how Paul looks at those he's won to Christ, this is an incredibly important concept to master. How we love our children, including how we discipline and forgive, is an imprint in their minds of how God works, and by showing firm hand, but loving kindness and forgiveness, the same as He shows us, we are demonstrating to them His love and grace. 

When we are resentful, angry, taking it personally and overcharging them, failing to forgive and forget, they may *be swallowed up with much sorrow.* It is how Satan gains an advantage in their lives. 

Many parents create a barter system in place of love, where everything is transactional. They may forgive but not forget, throwing past behavior at their child, holding it against them. Many parents don't even realize they are doing it. This behavior builds within a child the seeds for a works-based relationship with God, and can cause all sorts of problems in their lives. Never being "good enough." Never having healthy relationships with others. Never really understanding the charitable love and grace we have in hope through Christ Jesus. Maybe even bringing them to reprobation in the end, bitter against you and so bitter against God.

This is the division Christ brings. This is the reason for their hating us in His name.

<!-- post 4048 · 2022-07-30 07:20 · study -->

Paul has begun this letter to Corinth far less hostile than the last. His reproach cooler. 

His reframing of the question of letters of commendation to one of the Spirit and the heart of the persons saved by his or those he sent's ministry, that the believers' own salvation and behavior are the real epistles of the authority Paul and the others have is softer, and yet more biting. 

He spins from this to the idea of the letters of the law, of the old testament, being what condemned us, knowledge of the law bringing with it sin, which the Bible defines as transgression of the law (**1 John 3:4**), but in the Spirit is Liberty, the eternal life given in the new testament. 

This becomes a major theme for Paul in other epistles, the use of the law by God as a schoolmaster, bringing us to sure knowledge of our transgressions and death from sin, and the liberty found in Christ, which is more glorious than even the hand of God engraving stone and giving it to Moses. 

The veil Moses wore to protect the Hebrews in the wilderness from the lingering glory of God reflected in his face becomes a metaphor for the veil keeping the glory of God, through Christ, from the hearts of the Jew. 

But, with the veil taken away by belief on Christ, we see the glory manifest, and in that image, have it reflect in our own faces, the way it did to Moses, but unhidden and used to show others our faith that they might believe.

What a beautiful passage. 

"We faint not." 

And again, fitting with topics I've posted about in the past day, the reminder to look at the things unseen, not the seen, because they are temporal, and the unseen eternal. 

Paul writes that as we have obtained mercy in Christ, we have also received a ministry to share the light we've been given in that mercy. We are all ministers, servants to the glory of God. 

We don't hide the message, or teach it as "hidden things," gnosticism to be drawn out, but instead proclaimed in truth, in the light for all to see. Our making the truth known to be judged by others conscience in the sight of God. What we learn in the shadows shouted from the rooftops. Not hiding our light under a bushel. Amen.

That, of the Gospel be hid, it isn't due to our lack of ministering, but because Satan, the "god of this world," who deceives the whole world, keeps it hid to those that are in unbelief. 

We are to be servants to others for Jesus sakes. Because we were changed and born again because His sacrifice for us, we serve others in sacrifice for His sake. Being a servant to the Gospel to administer it to the unsaved is our calling.

Continuing the theme of the previous chapters, which of you recall, started regarding letters of commendation among men. Paul reminds that our letters of commendation are those who have believed because of our sharing the gospel. He says we are being judged by the conscience of the unbelieving, that our behavior should reflect the light that's within us, living as a light that guides them through the darkness put over them by the devil, winning them to Christ.  That we are ministers of the same Gospel of mercy that changed our hearts and brought us to focus on the things unseen, our conscience clear in Jesus. 

Building on this, Paul writes about our physical bodies perishing, but our glorified body from Heaven assured. That the Holy Spirit within us is the earnest, the down payment from God on His promise. Final chapter, Paul finishes his rebuke of the Corinthian church in stinging fashion. 

He says he's coming back, and in the manner established in God's law and Christ's teaching on church discipline, before 2 or 3 witnesses Paul will establish who is telling the truth, and who exactly it is that needs credentials to be established. That if he has to come there again it will not be to spare those who seek proof of his having Christ in him. 

He says examine your own selves, if they have Christ within them. If they think Paul doesn't, and that Christ is within them so mightily, sarcastically accusing them of not being saved. Of perhaps being reprobate.
