The Steadfast Faith of Obadiah Holmes: A Testimony of Perseverance in Christ

Let us consider the life of Obadiah Holmes, a man whose unyielding commitment to the Gospel of Jesus Christ serves as a beacon for all Baptists.

Born around 1610 in Reddish, Lancashire, England, near Manchester, Holmes grew up in a humble farming family. Baptized on March 18, 1609/10 at Didsbury Chapel, he was the son of Robert Hulme, a husbandman, and Katherine Johnson. As a youth, Holmes described himself as rebellious and wayward, immersed in "folly and vanity," admitting in his later testimony that he "minded nothing but folly and vanity... I was not only rebellious against my parents but against the Lord... continuing in such a course for four or five years." He blamed his disobedient acts for hastening his mother's death around 1630, an event that sparked a profound spiritual awakening. Reflecting on this, he wrote, "It struck me that my disobedient acts caused her death, which forced me to confess the same to her—my evil ways." This turning point led him to embrace repentance and faith in Christ, transforming his life from rebellion to devotion.

At age 20, on November 20, 1630, Holmes married Katherine Hyde at the Collegiate Church in Manchester. They had nine children, though their infant son John died in England in 1633. Holmes worked as a weaver and glassmaker, skills that would later sustain his family in the New World. Seeking religious freedom amid England's turbulent spiritual climate, the couple emigrated during the Great Puritan Migration, enduring a stormy six-week voyage to arrive in Boston around 1638. Tradition holds they brought the first tall-case grandfather clock to America. Settling first in Salem, Massachusetts Bay Colony, Holmes joined two others—Ananias Concklin and Lawrence Southwick—in establishing what may have been America's first glass-making operation, producing window glass. He received land grants, served on juries, and became a freeman in 1640, joining the First Church of Salem. Yet, even here, his growing convictions began to stir unrest.

By 1645, religious tensions mounted as Holmes increasingly opposed the Puritan practice of infant baptism, embracing instead "believer's baptism" by immersion—a hallmark of Baptist faith.

In his testimony, he reflected on this shift: "After many years in death and darkness, this good news of salvation brought light in my soul."

The Puritans viewed Baptists with deep suspicion, labeling them as "loathsome" heretics and "incendiaries of commonwealths, and the infectors of persons in main matters of religion, and the troublers of churches in all places where they have been." They accused Baptists of holding "other errors or heresies" alongside rejecting infant baptism, often concealing these until an opportunity to "vent them, by way of question or scruple."

Seeking a more tolerant environment, he relocated his family to Rehoboth in Plymouth Colony (now Seekonk, Massachusetts), where he became a freeman in 1648 and soon became involved in the local church under pastor Samuel Newman.

However, doctrinal differences quickly emerged, dividing the congregation over issues like infant baptism and church authority, with Holmes growing dissatisfied with the rigidity of the established Puritan practices. Influenced by Baptist ideas from nearby Rhode Island, Holmes and eight others underwent "new baptism" by immersion, marking their formal separation from the official church. This led to a prolonged schism that took about three years to fully manifest, splitting the church on doctrinal and legal lines.

Holmes emerged as the leader of this dissenting faction, known as the "Schismists" or "Schismatists," a group of about 14 individuals including John Hazell, Edward Smith and his wife, Joseph Tory and his wife, the wife of James Mann, William Devell and his wife, and others.

Clashes arose with the established church's pastor, Samuel Newman, escalating personal and religious tensions.

The Schismists were accused of setting up an unauthorized, irregular church meeting in opposition to Newman's orderly, approved congregation, holding separate gatherings on the Lord's Day from house to house, baptizing adults, ordaining officers, and breaking bread together—actions seen as direct challenges to Puritan orthodoxy and social order.

In October 1649, Holmes sued Newman for slander, seeking £100 in damages after Newman publicly accused him of taking a false oath in court—essentially perjury—though Newman later confessed in court that his accusation was based solely on hearsay from others, not personal knowledge, leading to Holmes winning the case.

The conflict intensified in June 1650 when a multi-part petition was brought against Holmes and the Schismists before Plymouth's General Court, urging that all such "schismatical activity" cease. On October 2, 1650, a grand jury—including Plymouth leaders like William Bradford, John Alden, and Miles Standish—indicted Holmes and his followers for holding unauthorized Sunday meetings from house to house "contrary to the order of the court" and promoting Baptist views. The court ordered them "to desist, and neither to ordain officers, nor to baptize, nor to break bread together, nor yet to meet upon the first day of the week."

Newman excommunicated Holmes, a severe ecclesiastical penalty that, in the theocratic context of colonial New England, carried profound civil and social implications. In Plymouth Colony, freeman status signified full citizenship, granting adult males the right to vote in colonial elections, hold public office, own and transfer land without restriction, participate in town meetings to shape laws, and serve on juries—privileges essential for influencing governance and community affairs. Unlike the stricter Massachusetts Bay Colony, where church membership was mandatory for freemanship, Plymouth did not explicitly require it; instead, candidates were elected by the General Court after demonstrating moral character, economic stability, and loyalty to the colony's Puritan-influenced laws.

However, excommunication for religious dissent like Holmes's often blurred the lines between church and state, leading to significant repercussions. It branded the individual as a moral and spiritual outcast, eroding social standing through community ostracism, loss of business opportunities, and familial strains. Politically, while not automatically revoking freemanship in Plymouth, it could prompt civil authorities to view the excommunicated as "opposers of the good and wholesome laws of the Colony" or threats to social order, potentially resulting in the denial of voting rights, disqualification from office, confiscation of property, or even banishment. For Holmes, this excommunication amplified the court's indictment, diminishing his ability to engage fully in colonial politics, defend his interests in legal matters, or maintain economic security, as dissenters risked being sidelined from land grants and civic roles. Amid the ongoing persecution, Holmes refused to comply, declaring his obedience to Christ above man-made laws.

In 1650, Holmes and his family fled to Newport, Rhode Island, a refuge for dissenters founded on tolerance by Roger Williams. Here, his Baptist convictions deepened, leading to one of early America's most infamous acts of religious persecution. On July 16, 1651, Holmes joined Baptist leaders Rev. John Clarke (physician and founder of Newport's church) and John Crandall on a pastoral visit to elderly, blind Baptist William Witter in Lynn, Massachusetts. The next day, during a private worship service at Witter's home—where "the word was proclaimed, converts were baptized, and the elements of the Lord’s Supper were served"—constables burst in, arresting the men as "erroneous persons" and "strangers" promoting heresy. The Puritan authorities condemned Holmes specifically for meeting at Witter's house to preach and baptize privately, for keeping his hat on during prayer in "disrespect to the ordinance of God and his worship," for receiving the sacrament while excommunicated, for rebaptizing those previously baptized (thereby denying prior baptisms as invalid and churches as nullities), and for denying the lawfulness of infant baptism. They charged that all this "tends to the dishonor of God, the despising the ordinances of God among us, the peace of the churches, and seducing the subjects of this commonwealth from the truth of the gospel of Jesus Christ, and perverting the straight ways of the Lord." A court found them guilty of "a half-dozen crimes of heresy," with Magistrate Encrease Nowell retorting to Holmes's claim of suffering for his faith, "No, it is for your error and going about to seduce the people."

Imprisoned in Boston for nearly a month, they were tried before the Court of Assistants. The court fined Clarke £20, Crandall £5, and Holmes £30—a sum equivalent to much of his estate—with non-payment yielding whipping. Friends paid for Clarke and Crandall, but Holmes refused, proclaiming, "I bless God, that I am worthy to suffer for the name of Jesus." He declined wine or comforts before punishment, resolving "not to drink wine nor strong drink that day, until his punishment was over," lest others claim the "strength and comfort of the creature" sustained him, instead showing reliance on God alone.

On September 5, 1651, stripped to the waist on Boston Common, he endured 30 lashes from a three-corded whip wielded by executioner James Smith with full force. Witnesses noted Smith spitting on his hands for grip. Holmes neither cried out nor showed pain but preached to the crowd, declaring, "The Lord, having manifested his love towards me, in giving me repentance towards God and faith in Christ, and so to be baptized in water by a messenger of Jesus, into the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, wherein I have fellowship with him in his death, burial, and resurrection, I am now come to be baptized in afflictions by your hands, that so I may have further fellowship with my Lord, and am not ashamed of his sufferings, for by His stripes am I healed."

As the flogging began, he affirmed, "Though my flesh should fail, and my spirit should fail, yet my God would not fail," and broke forth praying "unto the Lord not to lay this sin to their charge," feeling such a "spiritual manifestation of God's presence" that the pain was "removed" and "easy to me."

As the scourging ended, a bleeding Holmes turned and said, "You have struck me as with roses," adding to the magistrates, "Although the Lord hath made it easy to me, yet I pray God it may not be laid to your charge."

Holmes’s suffering echoes the trials of the early apostles in Acts 5:26-42, where Peter and others faced persecution for preaching Christ. The captain and officers, fearing the people, brought them "without violence" before the council, who were enraged that the apostles defied their orders to stop teaching in Jesus’s name, saying, "Ye have filled Jerusalem with your doctrine, and intend to bring this man’s blood upon us" (Acts 5:26, 28). Peter boldly replied, "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29), proclaiming Christ’s resurrection and exaltation. The council, cut to the heart, sought to slay them, but Gamaliel’s counsel prevailed, urging restraint lest they "be found even to fight against God" (Acts 5:39). Still, they beat the apostles, commanding them not to speak in Jesus’s name. Yet, the apostles "departed from the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name" (Acts 5:41), and "ceased not to teach and preach Jesus Christ" (Acts 5:42). Like Holmes, they faced imprisonment and scourging for their faith, yet rejoiced, seeing it as fellowship with Christ’s sufferings. Holmes’s cry, "You have struck me as with roses," mirrors their joy in persecution, his resolve to obey God over man reflecting Peter’s stand. So too, the Apostle Paul bore "in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus" (Galatians 6:17), enduring stripes for the Gospel, as he wrote, "Thrice was I beaten with rods, once was I stoned" (2 Corinthians 11:25), counting it all joy to suffer for Christ. Stephen, the first martyr, faced death for his bold testimony, praying as stones fell, "Lord, lay not this sin to their charge" (Acts 7:60), a plea Holmes echoed in his own affliction, beseeching God to forgive his persecutors. These saints, like Holmes, stood firm, their trials proving that "Thou therefore endure hardness, as a good soldier of Jesus Christ" (2 Timothy 2:3).

Governor John Endecott reportedly declared Clarke "deserved death and was worthy to be hanged," underscoring Puritan intolerance for Baptists, viewed as threats to social order. Roger Williams protested in a letter to Endecott, decrying the "tyranny and persecutions." The event drew international scrutiny when Clarke published Ill Newes from New England (1652) in London, including Holmes's account. English Puritan Richard Saltonstall criticized the colony's "tyranny and persecutions in New England," and it influenced Harvard's first president, Henry Dunster, to adopt Baptist views, refusing infant baptism for his son in 1653 and leading to his dismissal.

Returning to Newport in 1652, Holmes was ordained and succeeded Clarke as pastor of America's second Baptist church (now First Baptist Church in Newport), serving faithfully for 30 years. A respected civic leader, he acquired extensive waterfront land in Middletown along the Sakonnet River, shaping the area's development. In 1665, he subscribed to the Monmouth Patent for lands in New Jersey, assigning a house lot in Middletown (lot #20); sons Jonathan and Obadiah Jr. settled there, the latter becoming a judge and sheriff. During King Philip's War (1675–1676), Rhode Island's General Court sought his "advice and concurrence" as one of 16 "most judicious inhabitants."

In 1675, at age 65, Holmes penned a heartfelt letter to his children and 42 grandchildren, exhorting biblical living tied to their names—e.g., urging son Joseph to emulate the "bold" Joseph of Arimathea who claimed Christ's body. He reflected on faith's trials: "God in His own time chose a people to Himself and gave them His laws and statutes in a special manner, though He had always His chosen ones in every generation."

Holmes died October 15, 1682, in Newport, buried on his Middletown farm in the Holmes Burial Ground, his gravestone reading: "In Memory of the Rev. Obadiah Holmes Baptist Minister from Great Britain who died October 15th 1682 in the 76th year of his age." Katherine died shortly after.

His will, dated April 9, 1682, and witnessed by prominent Rhode Islanders, provided for his family while affirming his legacy.

Holmes's endurance advanced the cause of individual soul liberty, securing Rhode Island's 1663 royal charter under Charles II, granting tolerance that influenced America's First Amendment.

Remembered as an "eminent citizen—a champion of soul liberty," he is a direct ancestor of Abraham Lincoln through daughter Lydia. His Last Will and Testimony, a cherished Baptist exhortation, reminds us: Persecution refines faith, for "by his stripes am I healed." May we, like Holmes, count suffering for Christ as joy, holding fast to the promise that faithfulness endures eternal reward.
