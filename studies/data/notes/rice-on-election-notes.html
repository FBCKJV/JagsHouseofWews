Historical and Theological Context: Calvinism and Arminianism

Calvinism, often summarized today by the acronym TULIP (Total Depravity, Unconditional Election, Limited Atonement, Irresistible Grace, and Perseverance of the Saints), refers to a system of soteriology emphasizing God's absolute sovereignty in salvation. Its roots trace back to Augustine of Hippo (354–430 AD), who, after spending nearly a decade as a follower of Manichaean Gnosticism—a dualistic sect teaching determinism, total human inability apart from divine intervention, and a form of predestined election—incorporated similar premises into his later theology. While Augustine rejected Manichaeism's dualism, he retained and adapted ideas of inevitable human evil, unilateral divine predetermination, and grace that overrides human will, framing them monistically under one God. These concepts marked a shift from earlier church fathers' views on free will and were seen by some contemporaries as a "Gnosticized" heresy infiltrating Christianity.

John Calvin (1509–1564) drew heavily from Augustine but did not systematize the full TULIP framework himself; his theology was less rigidly extreme on issues like limited atonement or double predestination. The strict five-point TULIP formulation emerged later at the Synod of Dort (1618–1619) as a response to Arminian challenges, and it gained sharper definition and popularity in Reformed circles during the 19th and early 20th centuries, becoming widely held in modern American Protestantism, particularly among Reformed Baptists.

Arminianism arose in the early 17th century as a direct response to high Calvinism's interpretations of election and predestination. Jacobus Arminius and his followers accepted many of the same foundational assumptions—such as total depravity requiring prevenient grace and election tied to individual salvation—but conditioned election on foreseen faith—that is, on God's foreknowledge of who would believe—and emphasized unlimited atonement, resistible grace, and the potential “loss” of salvation. It sought to preserve human responsibility and God's universal love but shared the over-systematized framework that prioritized philosophical coherence on sovereignty and will over the Bible's plain emphasis on whosoever-will evangelism.

In the 20th Century, Fundamentalist Baptists came to prominence. Although there is an unbroken line of Bible-believing Christians—living faith to faith and rejecting both the theological systems of the Protestants and the vain traditions and dead works of the apostate Roman church—widespread prominence for such voices did not come until late 19th- and 20th-century America. Freedom from persecution, due to the religious liberty enshrined in our Constitution (which was added due to influence of early Colonial Baptists), allowed Biblical alternatives to these systems to be widely promulgated.

Dr. John R. Rice on election

Dr. John R. Rice (1895–1980) was a prominent Independent Fundamental Baptist evangelist, pastor, author, and founder/editor of The Sword of the Lord newspaper (started in 1934). He was a key figure in 20th-century American Fundamentalism, known for his emphasis on biblical inerrancy, soul-winning evangelism, premillennialism, separation from modernism/liberalism/worldliness/formalism, and the verbal inspiration and divine preservation of Scripture. His ministry focused heavily on revival meetings, soul-winning campaigns, and publishing materials that promoted New Testament evangelism and personal holiness. Rice's teachings on atonement, salvation, and election rejected both strict Calvinism (especially its "five points" or TULIP) and Arminianism as overly systematized extremes that distort the plain biblical emphasis on grace, human responsibility, and the universal gospel offer.

Key Aspects of Rice's Teachings

Atonement: Rice strongly advocated an unlimited/general atonement. Christ died as a substitute for the sins of the whole world, all of the fallen creation, providing a sufficient, propitiatory sacrifice (blood atonement) that objectively removes the barrier of sin for all humanity and makes salvation genuinely available to everyone. He viewed limited atonement (a core of Reformed/hyper-Calvinism) as limiting God's love, grace, and provision, essentially slandering God by implying Christ did not die for all. He cited verses like 1 Timothy 2:6, 1 John 2:2, Romans 5:20, and 1 Timothy 4:10 to argue that Christ is the "Saviour of all men, specially of those that believe"—potential/intentional for all, personal/effective for believers. The cross achieves real reconciliation and propitiation universally, but its benefits apply personally through faith.

Salvation: Salvation is by grace through faith alone, not of works, wholly on the merits of Christ's atoning blood. It is instant for penitent believers, involving regeneration (new birth), forgiveness, and eternal security once received. Rice emphasized personal faith in Christ's death, burial, and resurrection as the means of receiving eternal life. He rejected any notion of salvation by human merit, rites, or works, while stressing that believers walking in the Spirit, answering calls to election in service, will lead to changed lives, and soul-winning as the calling all believers have, bringing us unto perfection (that is, maturity in the faith, not sinlessness). He opposed both Calvinist irresistible grace (which he saw as removing free response) and Arminian loss of salvation (affirming perseverance/eternal security for true believers). Salvation is a free gift offered to whosoever believes, enabled by God's grace but requiring personal trust.

Election and Foreknowledge: Rice rejected unconditional election to salvation (Calvinist view) and conditional election based on foreseen faith (classic Arminian view). He saw election as God's sovereign choosing for roles, service, calling, or purpose—not synonymous with individual predestination to eternal life. Foreknowledge in Romans 8:29 is not God previewing who would believe and then electing them (Arminian), nor sovereign decree apart from response (Calvinist/hyper-Calvinist). Instead, it involves relational intimacy and God's plan for believers (corporate/categorical), assuring NT believers of the same outcomes seen in OT saints (foreknown, predestined to conformity to Christ, called, justified, glorified). Election is purpose-oriented (e.g., to holiness, service, or blessing), not a predetermined selection for damnation or irresistible salvation.

Overall Stance on Calvinism and Arminianism

Rice explicitly opposed both systems as "two sides of the same coin" or extremes that over-systematize Scripture. He labeled strict five-point Calvinism (especially limited atonement and unconditional election to salvation) as "hyper-Calvinism"—a false doctrine that limits God's love/grace, hinders evangelism by implying some cannot be saved, and contradicts universal atonement texts. He was not Arminian either, rejecting salvation's dependence on human initiative apart from grace, loss of salvation, or any works element. Instead, he urged following the Bible plainly: free grace offered to all through Christ's sufficient atonement, received by personal faith, with God's sovereign purposes in calling believers to service and assurance.

Rice's approach prioritized practical evangelism—urging sinners to repent and believe—while upholding substitutionary atonement, blood propitiation, and the necessity of faith in Christ's resurrection life. His writings (e.g., Hyper-Calvinism: A False Doctrine, Predestined for Hell? No!) and The Sword of the Lord promoted this balanced, soul-winning Fundamentalist theology, influencing generations of IFB preachers and churches.

Summary: This is the Fundamentalist view: objective/cosmic reconciliation through unlimited atonement by the death of Jesus on the cross, His blood on the Mercy Seat, and personal, individual salvation by faith in the risen Christ. Election is the calling to service or the predestined role, blessings, traits given to groups of people based on the criteria of God, often for all believers, rather than picking and choosing who is predestined to salvation.

In essence, the biblical Fundamentalist view rests on a straightforward trust in the Scripture as written—taking passages in their plain context and allowing the whole counsel of God to interpret itself—yielding unlimited atonement that reconciles the world to God (removing the barrier of sin and making grace genuinely available to all), whosoever-will salvation received personally through faith, and election as God’s call to service and holiness for all who believe. In contrast, both Calvinism and Arminianism begin with false premises about depravity, will, and God’s rule—premises ultimately traceable to Augustine’s incorporation of Gnostic ideas—then force those errors back into the text through eisegesis, redefining election, atonement, and foreknowledge in ways the Bible never intends. The result is a false dichotomy that pits a philosophical, micromanaging “sovereignty” (a modern term and concept foreign to the King James Bible) against man’s responsibility, when Scripture presents no such conflict. True biblical understanding rejects this man-made either/or and simply believes and proclaims the plain gospel truth: Christ died for all, His blood has reconciled the world unto God, salvation is freely offered to whosoever believeth, and God graciously calls believers to holy service.
