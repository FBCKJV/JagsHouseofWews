A Trail of Blood - A Brief History of the Baptists

33 AD–100 AD: Apostolic Era

  • The true church, founded by Jesus Christ, spreads through apostolic preaching. Early believers, called “Christians” at Antioch, practice believer’s baptism and local church autonomy, facing persecution from Jewish and Roman authorities for their adherence to Scripture.

100–313 AD: Post-Apostolic Persecutions

  • Groups like the Montanists (c. 150 AD) uphold biblical purity against emerging Catholic institutionalism, facing Roman persecution and opposition from the growing pre-Catholic hierarchy for their steadfast faith and early attempts to share Scripture in accessible forms.

313–600 AD: Rise of Catholicism and Persecution

  • After the Edict of Milan (313 AD), Constantine legalizes Christianity but orders persecution of churches refusing to submit to Rome or his self-proclaimed “Vicar of Christ” authority, targeting groups like the Donatists (c. 300s) who reject infant baptism and church hierarchy. The Catholic Church restricts Scripture to Latin (Vulgate), suppressing vernacular translations, with dissenting groups facing death, persecution, exile, and destruction of their texts.

600–1200 AD: Medieval Dissenters

  • Groups like the Paulicians (c. 600s–800s) and Bogomils (c. 900s–1100s) maintain believer’s baptism and Scripture-based worship, enduring Catholic crusades and executions. The Catholic Church, through local councils, condemns non-Latin Scriptures, burning texts and punishing those attempting vernacular translations.

1200–1500 AD: Pre-Reformation Movements

  • The Waldensians, founded by Peter Waldo (c. 1173), and Albigensians/Cathars (c. 1200s) preach biblical simplicity and translate portions of the Bible into vernacular languages (e.g., Provençal). Papal bulls, like Ad Abolendam (1184), order their suppression, with their writings burned and leaders executed. The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), launched by Pope Innocent III, targets the Cathars in southern France, with massacres (e.g., Béziers, 1209, killing up to 20,000) and sieges, resulting in an estimated 200,000–1,000,000 deaths. The Waldensians face intermittent persecutions (1215–1545), notably the Massacre of Mérindol (1545), with hundreds to thousands killed for rejecting clerical authority.

1500–1600 AD: Reformation and Anabaptists

  • Anabaptists (c. 1525) revive believer’s baptism and church separation, facing persecution from both Catholics and Protestants. Key events include the executions of Swiss Brethren leaders like Felix Manz (d. 1527, drowned) and George Blaurock (d. 1529, burned), and the Münster siege (1534–1535), where thousands were killed. Figures like William Tyndale (martyred 1536) translate the Bible into English, with their works burned and themselves executed under Catholic decrees like Exsurge Domine (1520). The French Wars of Religion (1562–1598) pit Catholics against Protestant Huguenots, with Anabaptist and Waldensian influences in some regions, culminating in events like the St. Bartholomew’s Day Massacre (1572, 2,000–70,000 killed), with up to 3,000,000 deaths across the wars. The Marian Persecutions (1553–1558) under Queen Mary I see over 280 Protestants, including John Rogers, Thomas Cranmer, Hugh Latimer, and Nicholas Ridley, burned at the stake. The English Reformation Conflicts (1530s–1550s) under Henry VIII and Edward VI involve executions of Catholic dissenters and some Baptists, with hundreds killed.

1600–1700 AD: Baptist Emergence

  • Early Baptists, like John Smyth (c. 1609), form congregations in England and the Netherlands, enduring Anglican and Catholic persecution. Their vernacular Scriptures face opposition, with authorities burning texts and imprisoning preachers. The Church of England persecutes separatist groups like early Anabaptists and proto-Baptists (1530s–1600s), with dozens executed and hundreds imprisoned or exiled. The Savoyard–Waldensian Wars (1655–1690), including the Piedmont Easter Massacre (1655), kill hundreds to thousands of Waldensians. The English Civil Wars (1642–1651), driven by Protestant-Catholic tensions, result in 100,000–200,000 deaths, indirectly aiding Baptist survival. In the American colonies, Baptists face Puritan and Anglican restrictions (e.g., Virginia, 1660s–1680s), with fines and imprisonments for preaching from vernacular Bibles. The Puritan persecution of Quakers in Massachusetts Bay (1656–1661) sees four Quakers hanged and dozens whipped or banished, with Baptists like John Clarke and Obadiah Holmes fined and whipped (1651).

1700–1900 AD: Baptist Growth

  • The Church of England vs. Puritans (1560s–1640s) sees thousands fined or exiled under Elizabeth I and the Stuarts, with figures like Thomas Cartwright imprisoned. Pilgrims fleeing such persecution land in Plymouth in 1621. Baptists in America faced continued restrictions, however.
  • Baptist Persecutions in Massachusetts and Virginia (1640s–1680s): Early Baptists, rejecting infant baptism and state churches, faced persecution from Puritan and Anglican authorities. In Massachusetts, John Clarke and Obadiah Holmes were fined and whipped (1651) for preaching Baptist principles. In Virginia, Anglican-dominated laws fined or imprisoned Baptists for unauthorized preaching into the 1670s.
  • The Great Awakening (1730s–1740s) sparks growth, with “New Light” Baptist preachers like James Davenport facing arrests for unlicensed preaching, though no executions occur.
  • Baptists uphold individual soul liberty and biblical authority, growing despite fines and brief imprisonments.
  • The Revolutionary War and the need to bring harmony to the newly formed states, many established by Baptist and Protestant sects fleeing Catholic and Protestant governments' persecution, adopted the Baptist idea of Religious Liberty, leading to the 1st Amendment's "Establishment Clause" banning a state run religion. Separation between church and state.
  • With this newfound freedom, the Gospel spreads and Baptist churches form throughout the nation.

1900 AD–Present: Modern Baptists

  • Baptists preserve the apostolic faith through local churches, maintaining access to the King James Bible, despite challenges from secularism, modernism, and denominationalism. Proclaiming the "Old Paths" and "Landmarks" that make up the distinctives we hold today.
