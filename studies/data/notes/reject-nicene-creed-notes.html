Critique of the Nicene Creed

Summary of My Rejection of the Nicene Creed

As an independent fundamental Baptist standing on the word of God alone—no church fathers, no reformers, no councils, no vain philosophy (Colossians 2:8), I reject the Nicene Creed outright. In trying to fight Arian heresy (the idea that the Son was created and not eternal God), the council of men in 325/381 imported Greek terms and logic that actually birthed new errors.

The Conflict: By calling the Father the "unbegotten" source and the Son "begotten" of Him, the Creed establishes a hierarchy of origin rather than of role. Even if they call the Sonship "eternal," the language implies the Son is derivative.

If the Father is the "One God" (the Monarchia), and the Son is "from" Him, the Creed essentially describes a "process," which by definition, becomes a characteristic of the Son’s creation, not a description of a static, eternal Godhead. The Creed uses Homoousios (of the same substance) to try to fix the "creational flow" problem this language introduces. But this brings another problem; the Bible doesn't say they are of the same substance, but fully and eternally the Godhead, one God Who is three persons. Not one God the Father and a Son made, or begotten, of the same substance by the Father.

Use of the word "begotten" (implying origin and derivation) denies the eternal nature of Jesus, while the use of "through him" for the Creation (making the Son sound like a secondary tool used by the Father), creates confusion.

Add to this the Creed’s assertion that the Spirit is issued from the Father and Son, rather than the Spirit being coeternally God as well, further complicates what the Bible describes. The introduction of this “Filioque” false dichotomy (Orthodox; Spirit issued only by the Father, vs Rome; issued by both) gives the unbiblical understanding that the Spirit, rather than being God, co-equal in the Godhead as God, is a created being.

All of this rationalizing use of “begotten,” "consubstantial" and "proceeds" becomes non-biblical philosophy of men, turning the Godhead into a chain of ontological origin rather than the biblical triune God.

If the Creed says the Son is “from the Father’s substance” (the very wording behind “begotten… consubstantial”), then by the plain meaning of language He is derived. There is no honest way around it. “From” denotes source. “Substance” denotes something He is taken out of or issued from. That is the definition of “made,” no matter how many qualifiers like “eternally,” “not made,” or “before all ages” you stack on top. You cannot escape the logic: something taken from another thing is secondary to it.

The men at Nicaea also chose to elevate faith in a visible "holy catholic and apostolic Church" and in their “sacrament” of water baptism "for the forgiveness of sins," to salvific essentials that must be professed, alongside their re-definition of Who and What God is.

The result? A creed that claims to defend biblical truth but subtly subordinates the Son and Spirit through implied creational hierarchy, rather than a hierarchy of roles, contradicts its own "not made" claim with creational-flow language, and adds extra-biblical requirements that damn by vain tradition. Creeds aren’t necessary. Scripture alone is sufficient: the eternal, co-equal, co-eternal triune Godhead (Father, Word, Holy Ghost, these three are one) needs no conciliar patch. The Creed didn't refute heresy, it dressed up a different version of the same root error, and passed it off as truth.

1. The Creed's Use of "Begotten" Implies Origin, Creating a Contradiction

  • Creed Language: "the Only Begotten Son of God, born of the Father before all ages... begotten, not made, consubstantial with the Father"
  • The Argument: "Begotten" is a term of origin or source, implying a "before" and "after." This introduces a creational hierarchy that contradicts the denial of creation ("not made") and suggests the Son is derivative rather than fully co-eternal God.
  • Biblical Support:
  • Scripture ties "begotten" to time-bound events, such as the resurrection, rather than an eternal pre-temporal state:
  • Acts 13:33 — "God hath fulfilled the same unto us their children, in that he hath raised up Jesus again; as it is also written in the second psalm, Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee."
  • Hebrews 1:5 — "For unto which of the angels said he at any time, Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee?"
  • Revelation 1:5 — "...Jesus Christ, who is the faithful witness, and the first begotten of the dead..."
  • Verses asserting the eternal deity of Christ without "begotten" origin language:
  • John 1:1 — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."
  • John 8:58 — "Jesus said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Before Abraham was, I am."
  • Micah 5:2 — "...whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting."

2. Framing Creation "Through Him" Subordinates the Son

  • Creed Language: "through him all things were made."
  • The Argument: While creation by Christ is biblical, the Creed’s wording makes the Son sound like a secondary agent or instrument of the Father. Scripture presents all three Persons as directly and equally involved ("by him").
  • Biblical Support:
  • John 1:3 — "All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made."
  • Colossians 1:16 — "For by him were all things created... all things were created by him, and for him."
  • Hebrews 1:2 — "...his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds."

3. "Homoousios" and Procession Language Introduce Non-Biblical Philosophy

  • Creed Language: "consubstantial with the Father"; "who proceeds from the Father and the Son"
  • The Argument: The Bible does not use terms like "same substance" or describe ontological "processions." These terms turn the Godhead into a chain of origin. Scripture teaches three co-equal Persons in one Godhead with functional roles but no ontological derivation.
  • KJV Support:
  • Colossians 2:9 — "For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily."
  • 1 John 5:7 — "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one."
  • Note: John 15:26 refers to a "sending" role, not an eternal ontological origin.

4. Elevation of the Visible Church and Baptism to Salvific Essentials

  • Creed Language: "I believe in one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church"; "I confess one Baptism for the forgiveness of sins"
  • The Argument: This equates institutional membership and water baptism with core doctrines of the Godhead, suggesting they are essential for salvation. Scripture teaches that the church is a spiritual body and baptism is a symbolic ordinance following faith.
  • Biblical Support:
  • Church as a spiritual body: Ephesians 1:22-23 — "...the church, Which is his body, the fulness of him that filleth all in all."
  • Baptism as symbolic: Romans 6:3-4 — "Know ye not, that so many of us as were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death? Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death..."
  • Baptism of the Spirit: Ephesians 1:13 — "...ye were sealed with that holy Spirit of promise"; Titus 3:5 — "Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost."

Final Summary: Why This Matters

In the end, the Nicene Creed's attempt to combat Arianism with human wisdom and philosophy (instead of pure Scripture) backfired. By forcing "begotten" onto eternity, it creates a logical contradiction: a term of origin ("source" and "result") while denying any creation happened. Framing creation as "through" the Son after calling the Father the primary "maker" subtly demotes Him to an instrument. Terms like "consubstantial" and "proceeds from the Father and the Son" (Filioque) introduce non-biblical chains of derivation, turning co-equal Persons into a hierarchy. Then it compounds the error by confessing "one holy catholic and apostolic Church" and "one Baptism for the forgiveness of sins" as if these are on par with the doctrine of God—elevating institution and ritual over the blood of Christ and Spirit regeneration.

Once you bind people to the Creed as a test of orthodoxy (as the council and every creed-affirming denomination does), you force them to swallow the whole package:

The Son becomes a kind of eternal demiurge—continually “begotten” out of the Father’s substance, never created yet never fully self-existent in the same way the Father is.

The Spirit is reduced to something “proceeding from” or “issued by” the Father and the Son (Filioque) or just the Father (Eastern version)—either way, not the co-equal, co-eternal, complete Person the Bible presents, but a derived breath or influence.

You must confess the visible “one holy catholic and apostolic Church” (their denomination or communion) as an article of saving faith.

You must confess “one baptism for the forgiveness of sins” as the act that actually remits sin, not the symbolic testimony that follows the remission already granted by faith and the baptism of the Holy Ghost.

None of that is in the Bible. Not one word.

This is the danger of creedalism: good intentions against one heresy open the door to others. Protestants who wave "Sola Scriptura" as a fig leaf while leaning on councils and fathers miss the point. I stand on the Book alone—the living God doesn't need men's improvements. The Bible reveals the simple, eternal Jehovah: three co-equal Persons, one God, no origins, no processions, no add-ons required for salvation. The Nicene Creed codifies error under the guise of orthodoxy. I'll take the KJV over Nicaea every time. Amen.
