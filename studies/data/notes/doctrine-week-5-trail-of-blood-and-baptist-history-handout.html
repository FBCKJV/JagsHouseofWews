TIMELINE OF MAJOR HISTORICAL SPLITS AND PROTESTANT DENOMINATIONAL DEVELOPMENTS

313 AD – Edict of Milan

  • Split from: Early Christian believers.
  • Reason/Founder: Emperor Constantine legalizes “Christianity,” but imposes his control leading to the Roman imperial church (later Catholic) institutionalizing and diverging from independent believers (including Anabaptists) over state control and formalized practices like infant baptism.

1054 AD – Great Schism

  • Split from: Unified Christian Church.
  • Reason/Founder: Disputes over theology ( clause), authority (papal vs. patriarchal), and cultural differences (Latin West vs. Greek East). Key figures: Pope Leo IX (West) and Patriarch Michael I Cerularius (East). Infant baptism methods (immersion vs. sprinkling) were secondary. Anabaptists, already distinct, remain separate from both Catholic and Orthodox.

1517 AD – Protestant Reformation

  • Split from: Catholic Church.
  • Reason/Founder: Martin Luther’s 95 Theses protest Catholic corruption (indulgences), promoting sola fide and sola scriptura. However, reformers like Luther retained infant baptism as a means of grace, contradicting sola fide by implying sacramental efficacy. Anabaptists, pre-existing and independent, reject this inconsistency.

PROTESTANT BRANCHES

1517 – Lutheran

  • Split from: Catholic Church.
  • Reason/Founder: Martin Luther, opposing indulgences and papal authority, teaches sola fide but retains infant baptism as a means of grace, creating a theological contradiction by tying salvation to a sacrament.

1520s – Reformed/Calvinist

  • Split from: Catholic Church.
  • Reason/Founder: Huldrych Zwingli and John Calvin emphasize God’s sovereignty and predestination, rejecting Catholic rituals like the Mass. They uphold sola fide but retain infant baptism as a covenantal sign, contradicting faith-alone claims by ascribing grace to sacraments.

1534 – Anglican

  • Split from: Catholic Church.
  • Reason/Founder: King Henry VIII, seeking annulment of his marriage (refused by the Pope), establishes the Church of England. Anglicanism retains infant baptism and sacramental views, conflicting with sola fide despite later Protestant influences.

1525 – Reformation Anabaptists

  • Split from: Catholic Church and mainstream Protestant (Lutheran/Reformed).
  • Reason/Founder: Leaders like Conrad Grebel and Menno Simons reject infant baptism for believer’s baptism (immersion), opposing state-church alliances and sacramentalism. They align with the continuous Anabaptist tradition, rejecting the Protestant contradiction of sola fide with infant baptism.

LATER PROTESTANT DENOMINATIONAL SPLITS

1600s–1700s

Presbyterians

  • Split from: Reformed/Calvinist branch.
  • Reason/Founder: John Knox and others adopt Calvinist theology, emphasizing elder-led governance (presbytery) over Anglican episcopal structure. They retain infant baptism, conflicting with sola fide.

Congregationalists

  • Split from: Reformed/Calvinist branch.
  • Reason/Founder: Puritan leaders like Robert Browne prioritize local church autonomy, rejecting centralized authority. They maintain infant baptism, undermining sola fide.

Methodists

  • Split from: Anglican Church.
  • Reason/Founder: John Wesley promotes personal holiness and Arminian theology (free will), forming societies that become a denomination. Methodists retain infant baptism, conflicting with sola fide.

Quakers

  • Split from: Anglican and Puritan influences.
  • Reason/Founder: George Fox emphasizes direct spiritual experience (inner light), rejecting formal sacraments, including infant baptism, aligning closer to sola fide than other Protestant groups.

1800s–1900s

Adventist Movement (1844)

  • Split from: Millerite movement (interdenominational Protestant).
  • Reason/Founder: William Miller’s failed prediction of Christ’s return (1844) leads Ellen G. White to found Seventh-day Adventists, emphasizing Sabbath worship and eschatology. Infant baptism is less central, but sacramental views vary.

Holiness Movement (late 1800s)

  • Split from: Methodism.
  • Reason/Founder: Phoebe Palmer and others promote entire sanctification (second blessing), focusing on holy living. Infant baptism persists in some groups, conflicting with sola fide.

Pentecostal Revival (1901)

  • Split from: Holiness movement within Methodism.
  • Reason/Founder: Charles Parham and William J. Seymour (Azusa Street Revival) emphasize baptism of the Holy Spirit, evidenced by tongues. Sacramental views vary, but some retain Methodist baptismal practices.

Charismatic Renewal (1960s)

  • Split from: Pentecostal influence, cross-denominational.
  • Reason/Founder: No single founder; figures like Dennis Bennett spread Pentecostal practices (tongues, healing) to mainline churches. Sacramental practices depend on the host denomination.

NEW MOVEMENTS ORIGINATING FROM PROTESTANTISM

Seventh-day Adventist (1844)

  • Split from: Millerite movement within Protestantism.
  • Reason/Founder: Ellen G. White establishes the movement post-Millerite disappointment, focusing on Sabbath observance and prophetic teachings. Baptismal practices vary but often align with Protestant norms.

Mormon / LDS (1830)

  • Split from: Restorationist branch within Protestantism.
  • Reason/Founder: Joseph Smith, claiming divine revelations (Book of Mormon), seeks to restore primitive Christianity. LDS baptismal practices diverge from Protestant norms, emphasizing ordinances.

Jehovah’s Witnesses (1870s)

  • Split from: Bible Student movement within Protestantism.
  • Reason/Founder: Charles Taze Russell promotes strict monotheism and eschatology, formalized by Joseph Rutherford. They reject infant baptism, aligning closer to believer’s baptism.

Pentecostal (1901)

  • Split from: Holiness movement within Methodism.
  • Reason/Founder: Charles Parham and William J. Seymour’s Azusa Street Revival popularize glossolalia and spiritual gifts. Baptismal practices vary, often emphasizing believer’s baptism.

Charismatics (1960s)

  • Split from: Pentecostal influence, cross-denominational.
  • Reason/Founder: No central founder; Dennis Bennett and others introduce Pentecostal practices into mainline denominations. Baptismal views depend on the denomination.

PATH TO MODERN IFB CHURCHES

The continuous Anabaptist tradition, existing since 33 AD, emphasizes believer’s baptism, church autonomy, and separation from state churches, rejecting sacramentalism and aligning with sola fide without contradiction.

By the 1600s, this tradition aligns with emerging Baptist churches, maintaining biblical inerrancy and independence.

In the 1800s, independent Baptist churches solidify, rejecting liberal theology and sacramental practices.

In the 20th century, Independent Fundamental Baptist (IFB) churches emerge, rooted in this Anabaptist-Baptist line, upholding strict fundamentalism, believer’s baptism, and local church autonomy, distinct from Protestant denominations and newer movements like Pentecostalism or Charismatic renewal.
