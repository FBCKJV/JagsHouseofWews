Fundamentalist View on Atonement, Salvation, and Election

Dealing with the doctrines of atonement, salvation, and election are approached with a strong emphasis on the plain, literal reading of Scripture, prioritizing biblical balance over systematic frameworks like Calvinism or Arminianism.

This view seeks to uphold God's sovereignty in providing redemption while affirming human responsibility in responding to it, without over-philosophizing the interplay.

The atonement as described in the Bible as Christ's propitiatory death on the cross—His shed blood serving as the ultimate fulfillment of the Old Testament mercy seat (hilastērion)—which objectively reconciles the world and all creation to God.

This work satisfies divine justice and pays the cost for redemption, a propitiation for not our sins only, but the sins of the whole world, removing the things that prevented God's grace from being extended to humanity.

Key aspects include: Objective and cosmic scope: The atonement accomplishes a real, foundational, cosmic in scope, total reconciliation (e.g., 2 Corinthians 5:19; Colossians 1:20), making peace possible between God and creation regardless of individual response. It is effective in this sense "even if no one ever believed," as it deals with sin's imputed guilt, the fallen world, the tally of the wages of sin - universally and individually - by providing the necessary condition for salvation's offer to all (e.g., 1 Timothy 2:6; 1 John 2:2).

Universal provision: Christ's sacrifice is sufficient for the sins of the whole world, enabling a genuine gospel invitation to every person, without limitation in extent or intent.

Substitutionary and propitiatory nature: While sharing elements with Penal Substitutionary Atonement (PSA), fundamentalism avoids strict Reformed theology formulations because the Bible emphasizes broad reconciliation over effectual application limited to a predestined group.

The cross is not merely potential but achieves an actual change in God's relational stance toward sinners.

This perspective integrates substitution (Christ dying in place of sinners) with broader biblical themes like victory over death, but centers on the blood's role in propitiation as the grounds for mercy.

Salvation: Salvation is understood as deliverance from sin's penalty, power, and presence, received by grace through faith alone in Christ's finished work.

It builds directly on the atonement's objective foundation:

Personal application: While the death provides reconciliation objectively (Romans 5:10—"reconciled... through the death of His Son"), full experiential salvation—eternal life, justification, and regeneration—comes through belief in Christ's resurrection and risen life ("saved by His life"). Faith is a free, personal response to the gospel, not irresistibly imposed or conditionally foreseen.

Necessity of faith: The atonement makes salvation available to "whosoever will," but it is not automatic or universal; individuals must trust in the crucified and risen Savior (e.g., Romans 10:9; John 3:16). We reject universalism while maintaining the gospel's general accessibility.

Holistic process: Salvation encompasses initial justification (forgiveness through the cross) and ongoing sanctification (transformation by His life, walking in the Spirit), leading to the ultimate glorification, which all believers are elect to at the resurrection.

The Bible stresses the simplicity of the gospel: Christ died for sins, rose again, and offers eternal life to all who believe, without requiring works or predestined selection.

Election is never presented as an individual, personal selection by God for salvation, we reject both Calvinist (unconditional predestination to eternal life) and Arminian (conditional election based on foreseen faith) models as two sides of the same deterministic coin.

Purpose-oriented: Election refers to God's sovereign choosing or ordaining for a specific role, service, benefit, calling, or future result (e.g., holiness, ministry, or conformity to Christ's image). It is not synonymous with being saved in scripture; Saved believers are elect unto certain things, but election itself pertains to vocation or purpose within God's plan (e.g., Romans 8:29–30; Ephesians 1:4). You have to be saved to be elect. Not elect to be saved. It is conditional, by faith. Unconditional election is a lie.

Election is corporate or categorical - emphasis: Often applied to groups (e.g., Israel as a chosen nation, the church as a royal priesthood) or to believers as a class set apart for holy living and witness (1 Peter 2:9; 2 Timothy 1:9).

Foreknowledge: In passages like Romans 8:29 ("whom He foreknew"), this is not God previewing who would believe (Arminian) or decreeing based on sovereign will alone (Calvinist). It may mean a broader determination that those fitting the category of believers will receive blessings (e.g., glorification), or specifically in Romans 8, a prophetic past tense assuring NT believers of the same outcomes believed by OT saints, the promise of what will happen to believers (to be justified and then glorified). "Foreknow" carries relational intimacy with God, especially the Old Testament saints had trusted, rather than the false idea of election by the foresight of “God knows who will believe!” determinism. Arminianism and Calvinism both start from a false premise of what these terms mean, and all their theology falls apart when compared with scripture.

Overall, this Fundamentalist framework promotes assurance for believers through God's faithful purposes, while preserving the mystery of divine sovereignty and human free will. It critiques overly rigid systems for potentially undermining the Bible's universal language (e.g., "world," "all") or the call to personal faith, aiming instead for a practical, evangelistic theology that encourages proclaiming the gospel to everyone.
