Adult Sunday School · The Baptist Succession

Baptist Names
in History

A study of the many names worn by the people of God through the long centuries between the apostles and today — and the one truth that held beneath every name.

Religious bodies rarely choose their own names. A name is usually given — by a leader, a town, a doctrine, or an enemy's sneer. Yet trace the names back far enough, and the same conviction keeps appearing: a church is a company of the saved, baptized upon a profession of faith.

Part One · First to Third Century

From Antioch to the Novatianists

For nearly three hundred years, one name sufficed. Then the church's purity began to be tested from within.

Christians
The earliest and simplest name, given at Antioch to the followers of Christ.

Novatianists
Followers of Novatian, who withdrew from a church he judged too lax toward those who had denied Christ under persecution — contending for a pure, regenerate membership.

Puritans
A name applied to the same separatist conviction — a church cleansed of unconverted membership, not merely reformed in ritual.

Cathari — “The Pure Ones”
Greek for “the pure,” this name traveled alongside Novatianists and Puritans in the third century and would resurface centuries later among the Albigenses — always marking the same insistence on an unmixed, holy church.

Part Two · The Schism Within

Traditores, Infant Baptism, and a State Church

The deepest wound to the early church did not come from Rome's sword alone. It came from compromise inside the church — and was then sealed into law by an emperor's embrace.

The Traditores
During the Diocletian persecution, some clergy surrendered copies of Scripture to be burned rather than suffer — earning the name “traditores,” those who handed the Word over. When restored to office afterward without repentance, faithful believers separated from them.
tap for verse →
Apostates
Others recanted the faith outright under threat of death, then sought readmission to church leadership without discipline — the very compromise that Novatian and his followers refused to accept, at the cost of being cast out themselves.
tap for verse →
Infant Baptism Takes Root
Alongside this compromise, the doctrine of baptismal regeneration spread, and with it the practice of baptizing infants — formalized as unofficial custom in the third century and later established by imperial law in 416 A.D.
tap for verse →
Constantine and the State Church
In 313 A.D. the pagan emperor Constantine ended the persecution — but what followed was no less costly. Church and state were fused into one institution, membership by citizenship replaced membership by new birth, and a hierarchy grew up to govern it.
tap for verse →
Persecution Redirected
Persecution did not end with Constantine — it changed direction. The sword that had once fallen on all Christians now fell on those who refused the new state church and its infant baptism: Novatianists, Donatists, and every group after them that kept to New Testament faith and practice.

Part Three · Third to Seventh Century

Donatists and Anabaptists Arise

Out of the traditores controversy and the new state church, dissenting bodies multiplied — each still contending for a regenerate church and believer's baptism, and each paying for it in blood.

Donatists
North African believers who refused fellowship with a state-favored church they considered compromised, insisting the church must remain pure.
tap for verse →
Anabaptists
Literally “re-baptizers” — a name of derision given because they baptized believers who had only received infant baptism, insisting baptism must follow a profession of faith.

Part Four · Seventh to Eleventh Century

The Paulicians and Their Heirs

Through the Dark Ages, dissenting churches persisted in Armenia, Bulgaria, and Italy — hunted, scattered, and renamed with every migration.

Paulicians
A dissenting movement across Armenia and Asia Minor, later driven into the Balkans and Italy, planting seed that would surface again as new names in new lands.

Petrobrusians
Followers of Peter of Bruys in southern France, who rejected infant baptism and the veneration of the cross.

Henricians & Arnoldists
Named for Henry of Lausanne and Arnold of Brescia, both of whom preached against clerical corruption and for a church gathered by conversion.

Part Five · Eleventh to Sixteenth Century

Albigenses, Waldenses, and Lollards

By the eleventh century the names multiply again — a sign not of confusion, but of a movement too widespread to be governed by any single hierarchy.

Albigenses
Named for the town of Albi in southern France, the target of a papal crusade in the thirteenth century for their rejection of Rome's authority.
tap for verse →
Vaudois & Waldenses
“People of the valley” — followers of Peter Waldo who preserved vernacular Scripture and sent missionary preachers across Europe from their Alpine strongholds.

Lollards
Followers of Wycliffe's teaching in England, translating and spreading Scripture in the common tongue at great personal cost.

Part Seven · The Mark That Never Changed

The Nature of the Church

Every name in this study points back to one contention, argued in every century against every pressure to abandon it.

“The nature of the church has been the most distinguished mark of difference between all of our ancestors and the Roman Catholic Church. The Montanists, Novatianists, Donatists, Paulicians, Petrobrusians, Henricians, Arnoldists, Albigenses, Waldenses and all of our Baptist ancestors have contended for the purity of the church. To them, a church was a group of saved people, baptized upon a profession of faith. Since infants can make no profession of faith, infant baptism was rejected. True Baptists today believe the same thing. The truth of the Lord's church is a great truth with far reaching effects.” — Hisel
Mark One
A Regenerate Membership
A church is composed of saved people only — never a mixed body of the converted and unconverted gathered by birth or geography.
Mark Two
Believer's Baptism
Baptism follows a profession of faith; since an infant can profess nothing, infant baptism was rejected in every century by every name.
Mark Three
Separation from the State Church
From Donatist to Waldensian to Baptist, this conviction repeatedly meant standing apart from — and often suffering under — a church allied with civil power.

Closing Thought

One Church, Called Out

Ekklesia — a called-out assembly. Whatever the century called them, and whatever the world called them in scorn, they held the same truth we hold: a visible, local, regenerate, baptized church, standing on the sole authority of Scripture.
