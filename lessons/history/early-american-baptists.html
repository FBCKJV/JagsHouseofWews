Baptist history

Early American Baptists

Establishment, disestablishment and soul liberty: three answers to the question of who rules the church.

Three answers

Early America asked one question: what should the state do about religion? There were three answers.

1. Establishment

The state backs the church with taxes and laws. One church (Cotton, the Mathers, the Anglican South), or all the Christian churches alike (Patrick Henry’s 1784 bill, Massachusetts after 1780).

2. Disestablishment, the middle way

No tax-funded church, but the laws, oaths and proclamations keep the state friendly to Protestant Christianity. John Jay.

3. Soul liberty

No state support and no state control at all. A church lives on the gifts of its members, and a man’s conscience is his own. The Baptists.

The Baptists fought the first answer and then pressed against the second. A friendly state, they said, is still the state in the church.

The long word

“Antidisestablishmentarianism” comes from the nineteenth-century British fight over whether to disestablish the Church of England, and it means holding that the state church should stay.

In early America the word fits the first camp. The second camp wanted disestablishment, but not the Baptists’ kind.

What they were fighting

Tax-funded churches

Everyone paid for the Congregational church in New England and the Anglican church in the South, whatever he believed.

Licenses to preach

A man could not preach without the state church’s permission, and unlicensed preaching was a crime.

Infant baptism as a civil duty

Babies were baptized into the church and the commonwealth together, and refusing it marked a man as a troublemaker.

Enforced conformity

Fines, whippings, jail and banishment for those who would not conform.

What they argued for

1Soul liberty. Every man answers to God for himself.
2Voluntary membership. A church is the saved, baptized upon their own profession of faith.
3No coercion in religion. Faith cannot be forced, and the sword is not the church’s weapon.
4Liberty for all. Not only for Baptists, but for every conscience, even those they disagreed with.

Render unto Caesar

They say unto him, Caesar’s. Then saith he unto them, Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s; and unto God the things that are God’s.Matthew 22:21

Two realms, and two kinds of authority. The magistrate keeps the peace. He does not keep the soul.

Let both grow together

Another parable put he forth unto them, saying, The kingdom of heaven is likened unto a man which sowed good seed in his field: But while men slept, his enemy came and sowed tares among the wheat, and went his way. But when the blade was sprung up, and brought forth fruit, then appeared the tares also. So the servants of the householder came and said unto him, Sir, didst not thou sow good seed in thy field? from whence then hath it tares? He said unto them, An enemy hath done this. The servants said unto him, Wilt thou then that we go and gather them up? But he said, Nay; lest while ye gather up the tares, ye root up also the wheat with them. Let both grow together until the harvest: and in the time of harvest I will say to the reapers, Gather ye together first the tares, and bind them in bundles to burn them: but gather the wheat into my barn.Matthew 13:24-30

Roger Williams leaned on this parable. If the tares are among the wheat until the harvest, then the sword has no business sorting them now.

We ought to obey God

Then Peter and the other apostles answered and said, We ought to obey God rather than men.Acts 5:29

The answer every one of these men gave when the court told them to stop preaching, stop baptizing, or stop meeting.

Thomas Helwys

The Baptist argument for soul liberty began in England, before any Baptist reached America.

1609
John Smyth forms a Baptist congregation of English exiles in Amsterdam, and Thomas Helwys is among them.
c. 1612
Helwys leads part of the congregation home to England, and publishes A Short Declaration of the Mystery of Iniquity, a plea for liberty of conscience that may be the first in the English language.
1612 →
He sends a copy to King James I. Helwys and others are thrown into Newgate prison, and he probably died there about 1616.
Let them be heretics, Turks, Jews, or whatsoever, it appertains not to the earthly power to punish them in the least measure.Thomas Helwys, 1612

The same conviction, a century and a half earlier, that Holmes, Backus and Leland would suffer for.

The New England core

1636Roger Williams banished from Massachusetts Bay; founds Providence.
1639–40sProvidence and Newport. The first Baptist churches in America take form.
1651Obadiah Holmes whipped on Boston Common.
1663Rhode Island’s charter guarantees religious freedom.
1770sIsaac Backus carries the fight against the tax-supported church.

Roger Williams

1635
The Massachusetts Bay court banishes him for “newe and dangerous opinions”, chiefly that the magistrate has no authority over the soul. He flees into the winter wilderness in January 1636.
1636
He founds Providence, a colony where no man would be compelled to worship.
1639
Baptized, he helps form the Providence church, often counted the first Baptist church in America. He left it within months and ended as a “Seeker”, so we claim his argument and not his later path.
1644
The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution argues that forcing conscience is itself the sin. John Cotton of Boston answers him, and the debate runs for years.

John Clarke

1640s
A physician, he helps found Newport and gathers its Baptist church. Sources date it anywhere from 1638 to 1648; it is among the first two Baptist churches in America and still stands.
1651
Arrested at Lynn with Obadiah Holmes and John Crandall for holding a private worship service, and imprisoned in Boston. Clarke is fined £20, Crandall £5 and Holmes £30.
1652
Publishes Ill Newes from New England in London, exposing Massachusetts to the English public.
1663
After years in England as agent, he secures Rhode Island’s royal charter from Charles II: “a lively experiment” in full liberty in religious concernments.

Obadiah Holmes

Born in Lancashire about 1610, a weaver and glassmaker, Holmes left Salem and then Rehoboth for his Baptist convictions, was excommunicated, and fled to Newport in 1650.

July 1651
Arrested at the home of blind William Witter in Lynn for preaching, baptizing and the Lord’s Supper.
Sept. 5, 1651
Thirty lashes with a three-corded whip on Boston Common. He declined wine beforehand and preached through the whipping.
1652 →
Succeeds Clarke as pastor of the Newport church, and serves it for some thirty years until his death in 1682.
You have struck me as with roses.Obadiah Holmes, to the magistrates, after the whipping

Holmes and the apostles

And they departed from the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name.Acts 5:41

Holmes read his stripes in the light of Peter and John. He prayed that the sin not be laid to the charge of his persecutors, as Stephen did.

Isaac Backus

1724–1806
Converted in the Great Awakening, he left the established church, became a Baptist in 1751, and pastored at Middleborough, Massachusetts, for fifty years.
1768–71
Baptists at Ashfield have their property seized to pay the Congregational minister’s tax. They petition the General Court and carry the case to the King in Council, which disallows the law. Backus takes up their cause.
1773
An Appeal to the Public for Religious Liberty, the plainest Baptist argument against a state-taxed church.
1774
Goes to Philadelphia with James Manning, president of the Baptists’ Rhode Island College, and meets the Massachusetts delegates to the First Continental Congress, to ask relief for Baptists taxed for ministers they did not choose.

Backus refused to take out the “certificates” that excused Baptists from the tax, because to ask the state’s permission was to grant its authority. He also wrote the history of New England Baptists.

Colleges, associations and Hezekiah Smith

Baptists in New England built the institutions to carry their case: a college, an association, and pastors who would travel for both.

1764
Baptists found Rhode Island College, later Brown University, under its first president, James Manning. Hezekiah Smith helps raise its support, spending eight months in 1769–70 in the South.
1765–1805
Smith pastors the First Baptist Church of Haverhill, Massachusetts.
1767
The Warren Association unites the New England Baptist churches and, in time, carries their grievances to the courts and Congress.
1775–80
Smith serves as a chaplain in the Revolution.

Daniel Merrill

A Congregational pastor in Sedgwick, Maine, and a convinced pedobaptist, who studied the Bible on baptism when Baptists came to town in 1802.

1805
His church votes unanimously to become Baptist, and Merrill, his wife and sixty-four others are baptized in the Benjamin River.
1808
Writes The Kingdom of Heaven Distinguished from Babylon, holding that churches that admit the unbaptized are not baptized churches.
1812
Miniature History of the Baptists: their origin can be found “no where unless it be conceded that it was at Jordan or Enon.”

Pennsylvania and the associations

Not every colony fought. Rhode Island and Pennsylvania offered liberty of conscience from the start, and Baptists there built the first networks.

1682
William Penn’s Pennsylvania grants liberty of conscience, and Baptists gather in and around Philadelphia.
1707
The Philadelphia Baptist Association forms, the first in America, and for generations helps the scattered churches stay together.
1758
The Sandy Creek Association forms in North Carolina, and Separate Baptists across the South join it.
1767
The Warren Association unites the New England churches, and by the 1770s it keeps a grievance committee to carry their cases.

The associations are why the Baptists could speak with one voice, and why their agents went to the legislatures on behalf of many churches, not as lone men.

Separate Baptists

The Great Awakening reached the South through the Separate Baptists, preachers who came out of the revivals and preached whether the Anglican parish permitted it or not. The older Regular Baptists were more formal and kept ties with the Philadelphia Association. The two groups united in Virginia in 1787.

1755
Shubal Stearns, a Baptist since 1751, leaves Connecticut and plants the Sandy Creek church in North Carolina on November 22, with sixteen members. From it the Separate Baptists spread into Virginia, the Carolinas and Georgia.
c. 1770
Daniel Marshall, Stearns’s brother-in-law, carries the work to Georgia, where he is arrested in St. Paul’s Parish and ordered to stop preaching. He preaches on, and settles at Kiokee in January 1771, where the church is constituted in 1772.

Their preaching was warm, their meetings were long, and the parish clergy did not like it. They preached under open sky and in houses because no one would give them a pulpit.

Jailed for preaching

June 1768
Spotsylvania County. John Waller, Lewis Craig, James Childs and others are seized and arraigned as “disturbers of the peace”. A lawyer complained they could not meet a man on the road without ramming a text of Scripture down his throat. They refused to promise to stop preaching, and lay forty-three days in jail, preaching through the grates.
1768–70s
Baptist preachers are jailed in Spotsylvania, Caroline, Chesterfield, Culpeper and Orange counties. Again and again they preach through the jail grates to crowds outside.
1770s
Lewis Craig and Elijah Craig, both jailed (Elijah in Culpeper and Orange counties), later carry their congregations to Kentucky.

Patrick Henry and the Baptists

Patrick Henry (1736–1799), the Virginia lawyer who cried “Give me liberty or give me death” in 1775 and later served as governor, stands on both sides of this story.

The friend

Legend has him pleading for these jailed preachers in a famous speech. That speech is apocryphal, written later by a minister, according to Semple’s editor. But Henry did act as counsel for some imprisoned Baptist preachers in Caroline County, and Semple calls him a friend in the fight to license their meeting places.

The opponent

In 1784 he backed a general tax to support teachers of the Christian religion, and the Baptists opposed it. See “Multiple establishment.”

He was a champion of liberty against Britain, yet he held that a republic needs the state’s support of religion. The Baptists thanked him for the first and opposed him on the second.

Reuben Ford

Born about 1742, Ford was awakened under George Whitefield at about twenty, and baptized some seven years later after hearing the Baptist preachers Read and Harriss.

1771
He gathers the Goochland church, with about seventy-five members, among the earliest Baptist churches in Virginia, and pastors it for the rest of his ministry. Dover follows in 1773.
30+ years
Clerk of the Dover Association.
Aug. 1785
Clerk of the General Committee at Du Puy’s meetinghouse, Powhatan County, which resolved against the assessment bill. The committee appointed him to carry its remonstrance and petition to the General Assembly.
1786–87
Reports that the assessment did not pass, and that Jefferson’s act on religious liberty passed instead. He and John Leland then served as the committee’s agents against the Episcopal incorporation act.

Not a brilliant preacher, wrote his biographer, but his simplicity, affection and a blameless life won the confidence of everyone who knew him.

Sources: James B. Taylor, Virginia Baptist Ministers (1859); Robert B. Semple, A History of the Rise and Progress of the Baptists in Virginia (1810). Ford was the clerk and messenger of the General Committee, not the author of its resolution.

The Anglican establishment

In Virginia and much of the South the Church of England was the law. Its parish and county officers did not merely favor it. They enforced it.

Preaching licenses

Granted by the parish and the county court, and withheld from dissenters.

Parish taxes

Everyone paid the Church of England’s tithe, Baptists included.

Legal penalties for dissenters

County courts, parish vestries and crown-appointed governors enforced it all.

Not always one famous name, but a whole system. The English Toleration Act let dissenters license a few meeting places, but Semple notes a few licensed places in each county was a small gain to people who wanted to preach to every creature.

The establishment side

They were not always villains. Many believed society could not hold together without the state’s support of religion.

John Cotton (1585–1652)

The leading minister of Boston. Defended a close church-state relationship in early Massachusetts, and answered Roger Williams.

Increase (1639–1723) and Cotton Mather (1663–1728)

Boston ministers, father and son. Upheld established church structures and the ideal of a godly commonwealth.

The Anglican system

Parish vestries, county courts and crown-appointed governors enforced the Church of England in the South.

Their motives were order and morality. The Baptists’ answer was that Christ is the only head of the church, and conscience cannot be commanded.

Multiple establishment

Patrick Henry wanted no single favored church, but he held that a moral republic could not stand without the state’s support of religion.

1784
The Bill Establishing a Provision for Teachers of the Christian Religion would tax every Virginian, and each taxpayer would name the Christian church that received his money. If he named none, it went to schools.
1780
Massachusetts’ constitution had already done the same, letting towns tax for “public Protestant teachers”, each taxpayer’s money going to his own denomination. Isaac Backus and the Baptists contested it.
Whereas the general diffusion of Christian knowledge hath a natural tendency to correct the morals of men, restrain their vices, and preserve the peace of society…Preamble to Henry’s 1784 bill

Henry’s case is civic, not gospel: Christian teaching is valued for good order. Scholars compare it to a public good that taxes should fund, like roads. That comparison is theirs, not Henry’s words.

This was the harder argument for the Baptists, because it sounded fair. Their answer: a tax for religion, even an even-handed one, makes the state the judge of what is a church.

The middle way: John Jay

The middle way sits left of Henry. Jay rejected a national church and a single state denomination, yet he believed civil order and liberty were tied to Christian morality, and that the states could shape their laws to keep that heritage.

1777
At New York’s constitutional convention he moved to bar Roman Catholics from land and civil rights unless they renounced the Pope. That failed, but his naturalization oath, renouncing foreign ecclesiastical as well as civil authority, was adopted and stood until 1806.
1795–1801
As governor he followed New England precedent in proclaiming days of Thanksgiving, and pressed for laws requiring observance of the Sabbath, the “blue laws” that closed work, trade and amusement on Sunday.
1816
Writes to Jedidiah Morse that “it is the duty as well as the privilege and interest of our Christian nation to select and prefer Christians for their rulers.”

Sources: John R. Vile, “John Jay,” First Amendment Encyclopedia (Middle Tennessee State University); on blue laws, The New York Times, Oct. 7, 1963, “The Archaic Blue Laws.”

This is the middle way in a single man. No state church, but a state that leans on Christianity and limits the civil standing of those outside it. The Baptists’ soul liberty asked more: equal liberty for every conscience.

Baptists and the Revolution

Virginia’s Baptists had been ground under the established church, and Semple writes that they were “to a man” favorable to any revolution that would give them freedom of religion.

Aug. 1775
At Du Puy’s meetinghouse in Powhatan County, the united Baptist body resolves to petition the Virginia Convention to abolish the church establishment and to leave religion to stand on its own merits.
Aug. 1775
It also asks leave to preach to the army, which is granted. Jeremiah Walker and John Williams preach to the soldiers, but find little encouragement and drop it.
1775–80
In New England, Hezekiah Smith serves as an army chaplain.

Mere toleration was not enough, Semple notes, since they had been jailed even where the toleration law was thought to be in force.

Virginia, 1776–1786

1776
Virginia’s Declaration of Rights, drafted by George Mason, guarantees religion in Article 16. Madison, then a young delegate, amends Mason’s “toleration” to “free exercise”.
1784
Patrick Henry’s general assessment bill (an assessment is a tax) would have taxed every Virginian for the church of his choice. Baptists petition against it through their General Committee.
1785
James Madison’s Memorial and Remonstrance helps defeat it.
Jan. 1786
The Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, drafted by Jefferson, passes.

Madison answers Henry

James Madison, then a young Virginia legislator and later the fourth President, answered Henry’s civic argument head on in his Memorial and Remonstrance (1785).

The Bill implies either that the Civil Magistrate is a competent Judge of Religious Truth; or that he may employ Religion as an engine of Civil policy. The first is an arrogant pretension falsified by the contradictory opinions of Rulers in all ages, and throughout the world: the second an unhallowed perversion of the means of salvation.James Madison, Memorial and Remonstrance, section 5
During almost fifteen centuries has the legal establishment of Christianity been on trial. What have been its fruits? More or less in all places, pride and indolence in the Clergy, ignorance and servility in the laity, in both, superstition, bigotry and persecution.James Madison, Memorial and Remonstrance, section 7

Madison argued from the good of the state. The Baptists argued from the gospel, as the slide “Different roots, same conclusion” shows.

The General Committee resolves

On August 18, 1785, the Baptist General Committee met at Du Puy’s meetinghouse in Powhatan County, with William Webber moderator and Reuben Ford clerk. Semple records its resolution.

…it is believed to be repugnant to the spirit of the Gospel for the Legislature thus to proceed in matters of religion; that no human laws ought to be established for this purpose; but that every person ought to be left entirely free in respect to matters of religion; that the holy Author of our religion needs no such compulsive measures for the promotion of His cause; that the Gospel wants not the feeble arm of man for its support; that it has made, and will again through divine power, make its way against all opposition; and that should the Legislature assume the right of taxing the people for the support of the Gospel, it will be destructive to religious liberty.Baptist General Committee, August 1785, in Semple’s History of the Baptists in Virginia

It urged every county that had not yet petitioned to do so against the engrossed bill, and appointed Reuben Ford to carry its remonstrance to the General Assembly.

Semple’s own judgment: the Baptists, he believed, were the only sect who plainly remonstrated, and the bill’s defeat owed much to them. Not all histories would put it that strongly.

The petitions

Madison’s essay was not the only answer. In the fall of 1785 a printed petition went through central and southern Virginia, and far more people signed it than signed the Remonstrance.

…that they are inwardly moved by the Holy Ghost to take upon them that Office, that they seek the good of Mankind and not worldly Interest. Let their doctrines be scriptural and their Lives upright. Then shall Religion (if departed) speedily return, and Deism be put to open shame…Virginia petition against the general assessment, 1785

Its answer to Henry was the gospel’s own: ministers are called by God, and the church needs no tax collector to keep it. Copies went to the General Assembly from many counties.

Source: quoted in The Heritage Foundation, “Did America Have a Christian Founding?”. The original forms are in the Legislative Petitions collection at the Library of Virginia. Whether Baptists wrote this one is not yet confirmed.

John Leland

Born in Massachusetts in 1754, Leland spent about fifteen years as a Baptist preacher in Virginia, and became the Baptists’ best-known voice for liberty of conscience.

1775
Ordained in Virginia, he pastors there until he goes home to Massachusetts in 1791.
1788
Tradition holds that Leland, who opposed the Constitution for lacking a bill of rights, met Madison in Orange County in March and stood aside for his election to the Virginia ratifying convention, on an understanding that Madison would seek one. The details vary by source, and Madison’s own public stance was more guarded.
1791
The Rights of Conscience Inalienable: liberty for all men, “Pagan, Turk, Jew or Christian”, not for Baptists alone.

Leland’s conviction was the Baptist one: the same freedom he asked for himself, he asked for those he disagreed with.

Different roots, same conclusion

In Virginia the Baptists found allies in Jefferson and Madison, who wanted the state out of religion for other reasons.

The Enlightenment wing

Jefferson and Madison reasoned from natural rights: religion is a private matter, and government has no jurisdiction over it.

The Baptists

They reasoned from the gospel: faith must be free, because a faith that cannot be refused means nothing.

Both pressed past the middle way, where the state still leans on Christianity through taxes, oaths and laws. The Baptists did not become secular for it. They asked the state to leave the church alone, so that the church would be free to be the church.

The First Amendment

Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage.Galatians 5:1
Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof…First Amendment, ratified December 15, 1791

The Baptist voices of Backus, Leland and the Virginia committee were among those that carried the argument.

In 1801 the Danbury Baptists of Connecticut, where the state church still stood, wrote to President Jefferson that religious liberty there was held as a favor from the state and not as a right. His 1802 reply spoke of a “wall of separation.” In their case it is a wall to keep the state out of the church, not the church out of society.

Disestablishment, state by state

1786
Virginia ends its establishment.
1818
Connecticut ends tax support for its Congregational church.
1819
New Hampshire ends its religious tax (some sources give 1817 or 1818).
1833
Massachusetts is the last state to disestablish, almost two centuries after Holmes was whipped.

The First Amendment bound only the national government at first. The states had to follow one at a time.

The thread

Holmes, Merrill, Backus, Leland and the rest are one thread.

  • Willing to suffer for believer’s baptism.
  • Unwilling to submit to state church authority.
  • Strong for religious liberty for everyone, even those they disagreed with.
  • Faith was a matter between a man and God, not a matter of law.

A line of believers

The Trail of Blood

These men stand in the line of believers who held the same distinctives and paid for them in every age.

Our inheritance

Soul liberty, the local church and believer’s baptism are not American inventions, but Bible convictions people bled to keep.

Handle with care

The phrase

“A wall of separation between church and state” is from Jefferson’s 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists, not from the First Amendment. For the Baptists, the wall kept the state out of the church.

The sources

Quote the primary text, not the summary. Popular quotes get garbled, which is why this lesson quotes Semple, Madison and Henry’s own words.

The limits

Soul liberty and the local church are Bible convictions. This history shows men who bled for them, not a system of succession.
