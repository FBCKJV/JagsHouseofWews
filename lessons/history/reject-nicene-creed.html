Summary of my rejection of the Nicene Creed

The word of God alone

As an independent fundamental Baptist standing on the word of God alone—no church fathers, no reformers, no councils, no vain philosophy (), I reject the Nicene Creed outright.

In trying to fight Arian heresy (the idea that the Son was created and not eternal God), the council of men in 325/381 imported Greek terms and logic that actually birthed new errors.

The conflict

By calling the Father the “unbegotten” source and the Son “begotten” of Him, the Creed establishes a hierarchy of origin rather than of role. Even if they call the Sonship “eternal,” the language implies the Son is derivative.

If the Father is the “One God” (the Monarchia), and the Son is “from” Him, the Creed essentially describes a “process,” which by definition, becomes a characteristic of the Son’s creation, not a description of a static, eternal Godhead.

Homoousios

The Creed uses Homoousios (of the same substance) to try to fix the “creational flow” problem this language introduces.

But this brings another problem; the Bible doesn’t say they are of the same substance, but fully and eternally the Godhead, one God Who is three persons.

Not one God the Father and a Son made, or begotten, of the same substance by the Father.

Begotten, through him, issued from

Use of the word “begotten” (implying origin and derivation) denies the eternal nature of Jesus, while the use of “through him” for the Creation (making the Son sound like a secondary tool used by the Father), creates confusion.

Add to this the Creed’s assertion that the Spirit is issued from the Father and Son, rather than the Spirit being coeternally God as well, further complicates what the Bible describes.

The introduction of this “Filioque” false dichotomy (Orthodox; Spirit issued only by the Father, vs Rome; issued by both) gives the unbiblical understanding that the Spirit, rather than being God, co-equal in the Godhead as God, is a created being.

All of this rationalizing use of “begotten,” “consubstantial” and “proceeds” becomes non-biblical philosophy of men, turning the Godhead into a chain of ontological origin rather than the biblical triune God.

“From” denotes source

If the Creed says the Son is “from the Father’s substance” (the very wording behind “begotten… consubstantial”), then by the plain meaning of language He is derived. There is no honest way around it.

“From” denotes source. “Substance” denotes something He is taken out of or issued from. That is the definition of “made,” no matter how many qualifiers like “eternally,” “not made,” or “before all ages” you stack on top.

You cannot escape the logic: something taken from another thing is secondary to it.

Church and sacrament

The men at Nicaea also chose to elevate faith in a visible “holy catholic and apostolic Church” and in their “sacrament” of water baptism “for the forgiveness of sins,” to salvific essentials that must be professed, alongside their re-definition of Who and What God is.

The result?

The result? A creed that claims to defend biblical truth but subtly subordinates the Son and Spirit through implied creational hierarchy, rather than a hierarchy of roles, contradicts its own “not made” claim with creational-flow language, and adds extra-biblical requirements that damn by vain tradition.

Creeds aren’t necessary. Scripture alone is sufficient: the eternal, co-equal, co-eternal triune Godhead (Father, Word, Holy Ghost, these three are one) needs no conciliar patch.

The Creed didn’t refute heresy, it dressed up a different version of the same root error, and passed it off as truth.

1. The Creed’s use of “begotten” implies origin, creating a contradiction

1. “Begotten” implies origin

the Only Begotten Son of God, born of the Father before all ages... begotten, not made, consubstantial with the FatherCreed language

The argument

“Begotten” is a term of origin or source, implying a “before” and “after.” This introduces a creational hierarchy that contradicts the denial of creation (“not made”) and suggests the Son is derivative rather than fully co-eternal God.

3. Homoousios and procession

The argument

The Bible does not use terms like “same substance” or describe ontological “processions.” These terms turn the Godhead into a chain of origin. Scripture teaches three co-equal Persons in one Godhead with functional roles but no ontological derivation.

Final summary: why this matters

Human wisdom backfired

In the end, the Nicene Creed’s attempt to combat Arianism with human wisdom and philosophy (instead of pure Scripture) backfired.

By forcing “begotten” onto eternity, it creates a logical contradiction: a term of origin (“source” and “result”) while denying any creation happened. Framing creation as “through” the Son after calling the Father the primary “maker” subtly demotes Him to an instrument.

Terms like “consubstantial” and “proceeds from the Father and the Son” (Filioque) introduce non-biblical chains of derivation, turning co-equal Persons into a hierarchy.

Then it compounds the error by confessing “one holy catholic and apostolic Church” and “one Baptism for the forgiveness of sins” as if these are on par with the doctrine of God—elevating institution and ritual over the blood of Christ and Spirit regeneration.

The whole package

Once you bind people to the Creed as a test of orthodoxy (as the council and every creed-affirming denomination does), you force them to swallow the whole package:

The Son

The Son becomes a kind of eternal demiurge—continually “begotten” out of the Father’s substance, never created yet never fully self-existent in the same way the Father is.

The Spirit

The Spirit is reduced to something “proceeding from” or “issued by” the Father and the Son (Filioque) or just the Father (Eastern version)—either way, not the co-equal, co-eternal, complete Person the Bible presents, but a derived breath or influence.

The church

You must confess the visible “one holy catholic and apostolic Church” (their denomination or communion) as an article of saving faith.

Baptism

You must confess “one baptism for the forgiveness of sins” as the act that actually remits sin, not the symbolic testimony that follows the remission already granted by faith and the baptism of the Holy Ghost.

Not one word

None of that is in the Bible. Not one word.

The danger of creedalism

This is the danger of creedalism: good intentions against one heresy open the door to others.

Protestants who wave “Sola Scriptura” as a fig leaf while leaning on councils and fathers miss the point. I stand on the Book alone—the living God doesn’t need men’s improvements.
