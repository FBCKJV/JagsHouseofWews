A Trail of Blood

A brief history of the Baptists: believers who held to the Bible in every age, often at the cost of their lives.

A line of believers, not a chain of churches

The Trail of Blood follows a simple, observable truth: in every age since Christ there have been believers living faith to faith, holding the same gospel and the same Bible distinctives, and paying for it in blood.

What history shows

An unbroken line of believers, saved by grace through faith, who held believer’s baptism, the authority of scripture and the independence of the local church, under different names in every century.

What it does not prove

An unbroken chain of one church, of pastors laying hands on pastors back to the apostles. That claim (Landmarkism, the “bride” doctrine) builds a rigid system on more than history or scripture can show.

We learn from this history; we are not married to a system. The line that matters is faith, and the Bible decides what a church is, not a chain of ordinations.

The gates of hell shall not prevail

And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it.Matthew 16:18

Christ promised His church would endure. He also promised it would suffer. The world has always hated those who hold His word without compromise.

And I saw the woman drunken with the blood of the saints, and with the blood of the martyrs of Jesus: and when I saw her, I wondered with great admiration.Revelation 17:6

The early church

33–100
Apostolic Era. The church founded by Jesus Christ spreads by apostolic preaching. Believers, first called Christians at Antioch, practice believer’s baptism and local church autonomy, persecuted by Jewish and Roman authorities for holding to scripture.
100–313
Post-apostolic persecutions. Groups like the Montanists (c. 150) hold to biblical purity against an emerging hierarchy, under Roman persecution, and work to put Scripture in the common tongue.

The rise of the imperial church

313
The Edict of Milan. Constantine legalizes Christianity, and the state church that follows persecutes the churches that will not submit to it, like the Donatists (300s), who reject infant baptism and the hierarchy.
313–600
Rome comes to restrict Scripture to the Latin Vulgate and suppress the common tongue. Dissenters face death, exile, and the burning of their writings.

The same pattern repeats for a thousand years: the Bible in the people’s own language, believer’s baptism, and no master but Christ, answered with the sword.

Medieval dissenters

600–1200
Paulicians and Bogomils. They keep believer’s baptism and Scripture-based worship, through Catholic crusades and executions. Councils condemn Scripture in any tongue but Latin, and the translations are burned.
1200–1500
Waldensians and Albigenses. Peter Waldo’s followers (c. 1173) and the Cathars preach biblical simplicity and put the Bible into the common tongue. The papal bull Ad Abolendam (1184) orders them put down.
1209Béziers: up to 20,000 killed in one day
1209–29the Albigensian Crusade: hundreds of thousands killed
1545Mérindol: Waldensian villages massacred

Anabaptists and the Reformation

Anabaptists (c. 1525) revive believer’s baptism and separation from the state church, and are persecuted by Catholics and Protestants alike.

1527Felix Manz drowned in Zürich
1529George Blaurock burned
1536William Tyndale strangled and burned

Tyndale gave England the Bible in English, and his books were burned under Rome’s decrees. Under Queen Mary (1553–1558) more than 280 were burned at the stake, John Rogers, Hugh Latimer and Nicholas Ridley among them. The French Wars of Religion (1562–1598), with the St. Bartholomew’s Day Massacre (1572), took up to three million lives.

Baptists by name

1609
John Smyth forms a Baptist congregation of exiles in Amsterdam; English Baptists follow, under Anglican persecution, their preachers jailed and their Bibles burned.
1651
John Clarke and Obadiah Holmes are fined, and Holmes is whipped in Boston, for preaching Baptist principles in Puritan Massachusetts.
1655
The Piedmont Easter massacre of the Waldensians, in the Savoyard wars (1655–1690).
1660s–80s
Virginia’s Anglican laws fine and jail Baptists for preaching without a license.

Liberty

Persecution drove believers to the American colonies, where the Pilgrims landed in 1620. In time, the Baptist conviction of soul liberty became the nation’s.

  • The Great Awakening (1730s–1740s) spread the gospel, and unlicensed preachers were arrested for it.
  • The new states, many settled by those fleeing state churches, adopted the Baptist principle of religious liberty: the First Amendment forbids a state church.
  • With that freedom, the gospel spread and Baptist churches formed throughout the nation.

The old paths

Thus saith the Lord, Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls. But they said, We will not walk therein.Jeremiah 6:16

Today Baptists keep the apostolic faith in local churches, with the King James Bible, against secularism, modernism and denominationalism. Not a new movement, and not an institution, but the old paths of the Bible.

Three great splits

313
The Edict of Milan. The state-aligned imperial church, later the Roman Catholic Church, parts from the independent believers over state control and practices like infant baptism.
1054
The Great Schism. The Latin West (Pope Leo IX) and the Greek East (Patriarch Michael Cerularius) divide over doctrine (the filioque clause), authority and culture: Catholic and Orthodox.
1517
The Reformation. Luther’s 95 Theses protest indulgences and preach faith alone and scripture alone.

The believers who never joined Rome were never part of these splits.

The Reformation’s contradiction

Lutheran (1517)

Faith alone, yet infant baptism kept as a means of grace.

Reformed (1520s)

Zwingli and Calvin: faith alone, yet infant baptism as a sign of the covenant.

Anglican (1534)

Henry VIII’s Church of England, its infant baptism and sacraments kept.

Anabaptists (1525)

Grebel and Simons: believer’s baptism by immersion, no state church. The consistent answer: if salvation is by faith, baptism follows faith.

For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast.Ephesians 2:8-9

Later divisions

1600s

Presbyterians (John Knox, rule by elders) and Congregationalists (local autonomy) from the Reformed, both keeping infant baptism.

1700s

Methodists from the Church of England (John Wesley, personal holiness), and the Quakers (George Fox, the “inner light”).

1800s

Adventists from the Millerites (1844), and the Holiness movement from the Methodists, with its “second blessing.”

1900s

Pentecostals from the Holiness movement (Azusa Street, 1906), and the Charismatic renewal spreading tongues across the denominations (1960s).

New religions

Latter-day Saints (1830)

Joseph Smith claims new revelation, the Book of Mormon.

Seventh-day Adventists (1844)

Ellen G. White, after the “Great Disappointment”: the Saturday Sabbath.

Jehovah’s Witnesses (1870s)

Charles Taze Russell: a “strict monotheism” that denies Christ is God.

But though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, let him be accursed.Galatians 1:8

Where the Baptists come from

Independent Baptists did not come out of the Reformation. They are the latest in a line of believers who never joined Rome, holding the same distinctives under many names.

Every age
Believers holding believer’s baptism, the local church and Scripture alone, apart from the state churches
1600s
Baptist churches by name, in England and the colonies
1800s
Independent Baptist churches, rejecting liberalism and sacramentalism
1900s
Independent Fundamental Baptist churches: the Bible, the gospel of grace, believer’s baptism, local church autonomy

A family likeness of doctrine, not a line of succession.

The B.A.P.T.I.S.T. distinctives

BBiblical authority. The Bible alone is our rule of faith and practice.
AAutonomy of the local church. Churches, plural: each self-governing under Christ.
PPriesthood of the believer. Every believer comes to God through Christ alone.
TTwo ordinances. Baptism and the Lord’s Supper.
IIndividual soul liberty. Every one shall give account of himself to God.
SSaved church membership. Believers, baptized after they are saved.
TTwo offices. Pastors and deacons.

What the distinctives mean

  • The New Testament says “churches,” plural, 49 times: each one local and independent.
  • The Pastoral Epistles give the qualifications for pastors and deacons.
  • Bishop, elder, overseer and shepherd are the same office: the pastor.
  • The offices are not priests: all believers are.
  • An ordinance is a practice the Lord commanded. It is not a “sacrament” that gives grace.
  • “Anabaptist” means “baptize again,” a Catholic insult: the converts had been baptized as babies, and were baptized again as believers.

Behold, hear, set thine heart

And the man said unto me, Son of man, behold with thine eyes, and hear with thine ears, and set thine heart upon all that I shall shew thee; for to the intent that I might shew them unto thee art thou brought hither: declare all that thou seest to the house of Israel.Ezekiel 40:4

The believers on the trail did what Ezekiel was told: they looked into the word, heard it, kept it in their hearts, and declared it, in the common tongue, whatever it cost.
