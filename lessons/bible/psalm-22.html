A study of Psalm 22 — the prophetic song of David that Christ Himself prayed from the tree

I. The Cry Itself

A Prophecy Fulfilled in the Ninth Hour

In the ninth hour, Jesus cried out from the cross the opening words of Psalm 22 — a song of desperation penned by David centuries earlier. It is recorded twice in the Gospels.

Matthew 27:46 ✥
Jesus cries with a loud voice, quoting the opening line of the Psalm.
Mark 15:34 ✥
The same cry, in the Aramaic Jesus spoke daily — "Eloi, Eloi."

The man Christ Jesus — after a day of betrayal, abuse, beaten beyond human recognition, scourged, shamed and mocked, cursed, hung on the tree, and made sin for all mankind — acknowledges in that moment that, having taken on all the sin of all creation, He is no longer looked upon by the Father with Whom He has been since everlasting.

Jesus cried out in Aramaic, the daily language of the people, the language He grew up speaking — conveying His humanity in that moment. The Hebrew words, long sung by Israel in the congregation, translated into Greek and given to us in English, never lose a scintilla of power.

Hebrews 12:2–3 ✥
Looking unto Jesus, who for the joy set before Him endured the cross.
II. Our High Priest

The Scapegoat, Once for All

Jesus, our scapegoat, received our sin in atonement, without the camp — taking it utterly away. Not as the blood of bulls and goats offered yearly for sanctification by the High Priest of the Tabernacle, but once and for all, shed for man. And He, as the true High Priest, entered into the Heavenly Tabernacle and applied His own precious blood upon the mercy seat in Heaven.

Consider

No greater love has ever been shown than this — that God became flesh and lived among us, sinless and perfect, that He might drink of this cup. Going to the cross that we might live. While we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.

III. The Ground of Hope

He Has Never Failed to Keep His Word

The Psalm turns from desperation to the certain hope of a holy, promise-keeping God. We cry out and feel He is far from us — that He doesn't hear, that He may have abandoned us to our plight. But there is always the hope of faith and trust, because those who trusted Him were never confounded. He heard the cries in Egypt and brought them out of the land. He heard their cries in the wilderness. He delivered their children to the promised land. He heard them in the days of the judges, sending deliverers to raise them up out of the evil their own sin had brought upon them.

Hebrews 12:1 ✥
Compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses.

We know God is good and cannot lie. Our deliverance is assured, even when the flesh is weak and we feel abandoned. His consistent faithfulness to those He foreknew gives us confidence and strength — these things were written for our admonition, that we may know Him as a rewarder of those who diligently seek Him.

Romans 15:4 ✥
Written aforetime for our learning, that we might have hope.

IV. A Shadow of Calvary

Through a Glass Darkly

In David's personal pain, we see a shadow of the suffering of Jesus — a reproach of men, despised of the people, laughed to scorn.

Matthew 27:39–43 ✥
They that passed by reviled Him, wagging their heads.

Compassed about by bulls, by dogs, men like lions — surrounded by Roman soldiers doing the work of the Jews, piercing His hands and feet with nails. Splayed out upon the cross, His joints distended and twisted to bear the weight of His own body. Death settling in His guts as He struggled to breathe. His bones in sharp relief against His stretched-out skin.

Crying out for water, Jesus was given vinegar mingled with gall — a bitter drink that could not quench His overwhelming thirst. The soldiers then parted His garments by lot.

Matthew 27:34–35 ✥
They crucified Him, and parted His garments, casting lots.

The desperation and loneliness Jesus suffered on the cross He had also carried with Him for thirty-three years — understanding, all along, the anguish that awaited Him at Calvary, while at all points touched by the infirmity of the flesh and tempted as we are. His prayer at Gethsemane, Son to Father, asked that the cup might pass — yet He remained steadfast to the Father's will, knowing full well the physical and emotional cost.

Romans 8:28 ✥
All things work together for good to them that love God.
V. From Desperation to Praise

The Condemned Man's Prayer of Certain Hope

Compassed by dogs, scorned, ridiculed, pierced and heartbroken, feeling abandoned by God — the Psalm turns to a prayer of certain hope. We know God has delivered every promise He has made. He has heard the cries of His people and, in due season, saved them. We may persist in prayer, reminding God — and ourselves — of His faithfulness, even as we ask for more immediate relief. His timing may not be ours, but at our weakest He shows His strength.

From the Text — vv. 20–21

"Deliver my soul from the sword; my darling from the power of the dog. Save me from the lion's mouth: for thou hast heard me from the horns of the unicorns."

From the proverbial horns of a dilemma, from the immediate threat, we call upon the name of the Lord — not only the saved, His darling, but the lost often find His strength in their time of trial. Foxhole converts. The Potter often moves the clay in such a way that we finally see our own shortcomings and impotency, and our need for His hand to lift us up, that we may become what He has planned for us to be.

If even Jesus Christ was made a reproach and murdered by wicked men, that we might know eternal life as the risen and triumphant Son of God — then our trials are an opportunity to overcome by His power and give the Lord the glory.

VI. Declared in the Congregation

Go to Church

"I will declare thy name unto my brethren: in the midst of the congregation will I praise thee." In the midst of tribulation, we shall praise Him — that is the mind of a mature Christian. It is difficult to see sometimes, and we may feel isolated, alone — but in our most desperate hour, we praise Him. We declare His holy name to our brethren; in the congregation we hold up His excellence.

A Plain Application

It may not be a primary — or even secondary — application of this Psalm, but consider: we cannot lift our voice in praise to the congregation, or declare His name unto the brethren, if we avoid church. If we forsake the assembly. Go to church!

The praise, glory, and fear of the Lord come from the knowledge of the doctrines of God — the understanding of His ways, the past performance of His promises, the teaching of the fathers through the inspired and preserved word of God. Faith cometh by hearing.

Romans 11:3–4 ✥
I have reserved to myself seven thousand men.
Hebrews 10:23–25 ✥
Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together.

That communal exhortation is invaluable to a believer in tribulation. Hearing the victory Jesus has given others provides the strength to go on — to know the meek shall eat and be satisfied, seeking the Lord and finding Him, unto eternal life.

John 6:35–37 ✥
I am the bread of life; him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out.
VII. To Every Generation

A People Not Yet Born

The truth of this gospel goes to the ends of the world, and every knee shall bow — for the kingdom is the LORD's, and He is governor among the nations. Our hope rests on the truth that what God has promised to deliver, He will. The present suffering pales in comparison to what is to come.

Romans 8:18 ✥
Not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us.

"A seed shall serve him; it shall be accounted to the Lord for a generation. They shall come, and shall declare his righteousness unto a people that shall be born, that he hath done this." Our suffering becomes cause to praise the Lord before the brethren, to bring others to the knowledge of Christ, to proclaim His power and promise, and to partake in the spreading of His gospel to all people — that every tongue shall confess that He hath done this.

For Class Discussion

  1. Why is it significant that Jesus quoted this Psalm in Aramaic — the language of His everyday life — rather than in formal Hebrew?
  2. Verses 16 and 18 describe pierced hands and feet and garments divided by lot, written roughly a thousand years before crucifixion existed as a Roman practice. What does this tell us about the nature of prophecy and the authorship of Scripture?
  3. The Psalm moves from "why hast thou forsaken me" to "I will declare thy name unto my brethren." What causes that turn, and where do we see the same pattern in our own trials?
  4. How does Hebrews 10:23–25 connect the Psalm's call to praise "in the midst of the congregation" to our own responsibility to gather with the local church?
  5. In what sense was Jesus a "foxhole convert's" answer and also the Savior of "His darling" — both the lost and the saved, in verse 20?
