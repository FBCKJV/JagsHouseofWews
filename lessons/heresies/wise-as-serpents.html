Wise as Serpents

A Sunday School Lesson

Wise as Serpents

Handling doubtful disputations and sowers of division

Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves. Matthew 10:16

I

Two commands, not one

Jesus sends His disciples out as sheep among wolves, and in the same breath He gives them two charges: be wise as serpents, and be harmless as doves. Many Christians keep the second and quietly drop the first.

We are good at being doves. We are not nearly as good at being serpents — at understanding the context and nature of those who come into our lives and exert a negative influence.

Cross references

DiscussWhy would Jesus pair these two qualities? What does a Christian look like who has only one of them?

II

The dove-only trap

We feel the need to show grace, to bridge peace, and to avoid conflict in every situation — to be sheep in both mind and spirit. The wolves take advantage of this.

Part of the problem is that we can’t imagine how evil people might behave. We cut them slack. We extend grace on our own conscience’s terms, and show a kindness and mercy that only encourages and emboldens them.

The wolf rarely comes with an obvious lie. He comes with a grain of truth. In the interest of fairness, and to seem reasonable, we validate his premise — but the premise was offered in bad faith. He was never seeking dialogue or understanding. His intent is to throw bombs, sow discord, and validate slander to promote his own agenda.

Grace without discernment is not biblical peace; it is an open door.

Cross references

DiscussThink of a time a “reasonable” point turned out to be a wedge. What was the grain of truth, and what was the real aim?

III

Weak brother or wolf?

Not everyone who comes in wrong is a wolf. Some are weak in the faith — confused on a doctrinal point, turned around by something they heard, or convicted about something the Bible doesn’t explicitly teach.

Those we receive. We bear with the ignorant, and we correct them in love. But Paul attaches a limit to the receiving: not to doubtful disputations. When a man comes in to argue, to stir turmoil, and to break the unity of the brethren, he is not to be received on those terms.

The difference is the intent of the heart. That is what the serpent’s wisdom is for.

Cross references

DiscussWhat signs tell you someone is asking to learn, and what signs tell you they are looking for a fight?

IV

First and second admonition

Even the one who is simply in error gets a limit. Admonish him kindly. Admonish him again. If he is too proud to accept rebuke, the Bible does not tell you to keep the conversation going forever. It tells you to reject him.

Notice what Paul puts right before that command: foolish questions, genealogies, contentions, and strivings — unprofitable and vain. The church has no obligation to keep hosting them.

Matthew 18 gives the order: go to him alone, then with witnesses, then before the church. If he will not hear the church, he is to be treated as a heathen man and a publican.

Cross references

DiscussWhy does God give a limit to admonition instead of telling us to keep reasoning indefinitely?

V

Answering the slanderer

Defending yourself against a false accusation built on a bad-faith premise does nothing but legitimize the other person’s point.

If someone accused you of something vile and absurd about your own mother, your answer should not be “I am not!” It should be “You’re wicked!” You don’t defend yourself from slander by reasoning with the slanderer. You identify what he is doing, and you rebuke it.

This is especially true at the door when soul winning. It does no one any good to get bogged down in a doubtful disputation. When the hearer is plainly after contention and not the truth, shake off the dust and go to the next door.

Cross references

DiscussProverbs 26 gives both answers back to back. When do we refuse to engage the folly, and when do we expose it?

VI

It will eat as doth a canker

The real damage comes when sowers of division are entertained in the house of God or in a place of fellowship, and not put out at the first sign of their behavior.

Their contention grows and draws others in. Sheep who aren’t yet wise as serpents are pressured to compromise the truth to bring peace — to smooth things over, to debate slander as if it had merit. That only encourages the wolves, and keeps the confused, weak brother in his error.

It doesn’t matter what form the vain babbling takes: denying the resurrection to come, slandering men of God and their works, discouraging first love and soul winning, or teaching a false gospel that says men are made elect by their genealogy. It increases ungodliness and overthrows the faith of some.

Cross references

DiscussGod names the sower of discord alongside a lying tongue and hands that shed innocent blood. Why do you think it ranks so seriously with Him?

VII

Where the line is drawn

Serpent and dove are not opposites to balance. They work together: see clearly, act rightly, and keep your own heart clean while you do it.

  1. Error, with humility and teachability

    Patient instruction

  2. Error, with pride and refusal after admonition

    Reject

  3. Discord sown by design

    Identify, remove the influence, and treat as an outsider once the process is complete

  4. At the door, soul winning

    Don’t be drawn into disputations — shake the dust when they want contention, not truth

Many Christians default to dove-only behavior because they cannot imagine the calculated malice, or the strategic use of partial truth, that wolves employ. Biblical wisdom requires us to see it when it appears and to act accordingly — without losing the innocence of the dove.

Grace without discernment is not biblical peace; it is an open door.

Take it homeWhich comes more naturally to you, the serpent or the dove? What is one way you’ll practice the one that doesn’t?
