God's gospel is simple

God's gospel is simple: grace through faith in Christ's finished work, which not only saves us from Hell and gives us eternal life in Jesus, the Spirit indwelling us, quickening our spirit, sealing us unto redemption, but God also graciously empowers us to choose obedience, His paths, His commandments, allowing us to strive for maturity in Him, and live separated lives as the New Testament demands.

Complicated, twisted, and poisoned

But over the centuries, and even today, that simplicity has been complicated, twisted, and poisoned by human systems that shift blame from personal choice to inherited inability or divine decree, and make grace no more grace.

Leading men to destruction, and to pridefully destroy those that remind them of their error.

Early 5th century

A major turning point

A major turning point came in the early 5th century with the clash between Augustine of Hippo and Pelagius, the ascetic monk from Britain.

A constructed strawman

Augustine's doctrines of Manichaean gnostic determinism, which he championed (including his novel theory of "original sin" as inherited corruption, "prevenient/irresistible grace" as the sole cause of good, and redefining predestination as preordainment) were advanced by redefining orthodoxy against a constructed "Pelagian" strawman opposite, and attributing to Pelagius heresies which were not held by the ascetic monk in Britain.

A Hegelian dialectic

This approach functioned as a Hegelian dialectic: Augustine pitted his views against an exaggerated opposite to legitimize them as the necessary middle ground.

By securing Pelagius's condemnation through imperial and ecclesiastical pressure, Augustine's theology gained dominance in the emerging Roman Catholic Church, influenced by political patrons in the late Empire.

Made anathema

This pressure of being made anathema and labeled a heretic served to make others who held the Eastern, Greek traditions of New Testament Christianity and understanding fearful of being similarly labeled.

The writings of Pelagius and others who held his actual beliefs were either destroyed or allowed to fall into obscurity due to this fear, and so the lack of an extensive record lends even more credibility to Augustine's false doctrines.

Not a settled orthodox doctrine

Regarding original sin, Augustine's formulation—where Adam's sin transmits inherited guilt and an inevitable inclination to evil from birth—was not a settled orthodox doctrine in the earlier churches.

Pre-Augustinian Christian asceticism generally viewed human nature as inclined toward goodness, if incapable of being good outside of grace entered into by faith. That man is capable of moral progress in Christ, with sin arising from personal choices, habits, or imitation of bad examples rather than a deterministic corruption inherited biologically or legally.

Grace and the Spirit's work as essential

Despite the smears, Pelagius explicitly affirmed God's grace and the Spirit's work as essential: grace opens the heart, reveals truth, exposes snares, enlightens with heavenly gifts, and forgives sins gratuitously.

In his Letter to Demetrias and other texts, he described virtue as resulting from cooperation—human consent and effort partnering with divine aid to battle sin and imitate Christ's example of holy living.

Not sinless perfection

This was not sinless perfection by fleshly will alone (he acknowledged ongoing weakness and the need for maturity, or perfection in Christ), nor salvation by Old Testament law-keeping (which he recognized all fall short of), but pursuit of Christ's new commandment: loving God fully, with all your heart and loving your neighbor as yourself, all enabled by grace in the regenerated life of the believer.

Grace-enabled free agency

Rather than portray Pelagius as claiming flawless, sinless perfection or denying God's grace and human weakness/sin, we see a view of grace-enabled free agency and moral responsibility that was orthodox into the late 4th/early 5th-century Christian theology, particularly in the ascetic context—before Augustine's influence shifted the boundaries, and changed the conversation to say men are beyond responsibility, and can only be as "good" as God predetermined they be.

The Eastern church

In the Eastern church, again predominantly Greek, Antioch tradition, they had grown to see the natural end result of following the New Testament was living simply, loving others, being charitable, not given to drink, gluttony, fornication, adultery, coveting, etc. To put others' profit above your own.

This contrasted with late empire libertine culture in Rome, and the gnostic influence from Alexandria, which saw excess, and to the ascetic mind, sinful living being supported by the religious leaders there.

Augustine diverged

Augustine, from North Africa with roots in Alexandrian thought, a deep Manichaean past, and limited Greek proficiency, which introduced interpretive errors and conclusions, because he lacked the ability to understand the Greek texts. So Augustine diverged from earlier traditions, and his heresies have over time become foundational for many supposed Christians.

Pelagius's outspoken criticism of Roman hedonism and clerical hypocrisy made him unpopular among those Augustine later influenced to support his theology and anathematize his critic.

A reevaluation

This reevaluation of the false charges of "Pelagianism," isn't meant to idealize Pelagius but recognize that the traditional "heretic" label stems more from polemical and political wrangling by Augustine and others, than from his actual teachings.

In that context, we should reconsider how Augustine's legacy shaped Western Christianity—often at the expense of biblical exegesis and understanding of choice, responsibility, and grace-empowered obedience.

Complicated by systematics of men

  • That the simplicity of the Gospel and simplicity of life we should strive for, has been complicated by systematics of men.
  • That the separated life and sanctification believers should strive for has been abandoned by many professing Christians.
  • And that the teachings on lack of responsibility, or "inability to respond," has allowed those influenced by Augustine to fall well short of the potential given us by grace through faith, and the example of Christ we should actually be striving for.

The pattern is clear

The pattern is clear once you see it.

Groups that actually try to live the separated, simple, holy life the New Testament calls for—loving God with all the heart, loving neighbor as self, putting others first, avoiding the flesh’s excesses—have always drawn persecution from the powers that be.

The "pure ones"

The Cathars

Look at the Cathars (the "pure ones"): extreme ascetics who rejected Roman wealth, hierarchy, sacraments, and papal authority in favor of purity and spiritual living.

Rome branded them dualist heretics with false charges and launched a full crusade to wipe them out, massacring thousands in genocidal campaigns like the siege of Béziers in 1209, where "Kill them all, God will know His own" became the motto.

Their crime? Living too separated from the corruption they saw in the Church, exposing the hypocrisy just like Pelagius did.

"Poor men of Lyon"

The Waldensians

The Waldensians ("poor men of Lyon") did the same in a more biblical way: lay preaching, poverty, charity, vernacular Scripture (KJV-like emphasis on the Word for all), no oaths or excess, and rejection of papal authority and indulgences. They wanted apostolic simplicity.

Rome excommunicated them, hunted them with crusades and Inquisition, burning them at stakes, drowning families, and slaughtering villages in the Alps—murderous false witness calling them heretics for rejecting infant baptism, purgatory, and clerical pomp.

Why? Because their separated life exposed clerical hypocrisy, echoing the smears Augustine used on Pelagius.

Through the Reformers

This false witness—strawmanning true believers as heretics to justify murder—didn't stop with Augustine and Rome. It continued through the Reformers:

  • Luther labeled Anabaptists "fanatics" and supported their execution;
  • Calvin had Servetus burned and drowned Anabaptists in Geneva for rebaptism;
  • Zwingli oversaw the drowning of believers in Zurich rivers;
  • Anglicans imprisoned and executed Puritans and early Baptists for nonconformity.
1553–1558

Bloody Mary

Even "Bloody Mary" (Queen Mary I of England) burned hundreds of Protestants at the stake during her reign (1553–1558), including men, women, and children who rejected papal authority and held to Scripture alone—false witness and torture to enforce high-church conformity.

Paulicians, Bogomils and others

Add in the Paulicians: 7th-century ascetics rejecting icons, hierarchy, sacraments, and papal-like authority, emphasizing Scripture and simple faith—they were massacred by Byzantine emperors and labeled Manichaean heretics (ironic, given Augustine's past).

The Bogomils followed, dualistic but ascetic, persecuted by Orthodox and Catholic alike with burnings and exiles. Petrobrusians and Henricians (12th century) preached against infant baptism and corruption, only to be burned alive. Arnoldists rejected clerical wealth—hunted down.

The Lollards

And the Lollards, followers of Wycliffe, pushed Bible in English, poverty, holy living, and rejection of papal authority—branded heretics, burned at stakes under English law, their bones dug up and scattered.

Even quasi-Augustinian sects like the Puritans and Plymouth Brethren, who pursued disciplined, world-denying holiness—plain living, strict Sabbath, moral rigor—faced hostility. Puritans fled England under Anglican pressure, facing starvation and native attacks; Brethren separated from established bodies and were often mocked or marginalized, even as they carried some Calvinist errors.

Purity draws fire

Rome and high-church Protestant systems alike use political power to silence those who live too close to Christ’s example. The simple gospel—grace through faith enabling obedience, choice, and separated living—threatens any institution that has grown fat on compromise.

When believers start striving for the real thing, the powers that profit from laxity always push back with false witness and murder. History proves it: purity draws fire, leaving a trail of blood from NT martyrs through these groups to the burnings under Bloody Mary and beyond.

The pattern hasn't changed

Augustine’s legacy perverted the gospel and weakened responsibility which excused the excess and rationalized the evil done to separated Christians with standards; the faithful who keep trying to live Biblical lives and are persecuted for it.

The pattern hasn’t changed.

All that will live godly

The Bible says,

Yea, and all that will live godly in Christ Jesus shall suffer persecution.2 Timothy 3:12

Jesus warns again and again that Christians will be hated for His name. That we will suffer persecution. Paul, Peter, John and Jude all write about scoffers, mockers, persecution for the faith, and the patience of the saints who endure it.

Free to worship

We count ourselves blessed that in America, today, we aren't facing any real persecution. That we are free to worship, pray, read and own the Bible, go soul-winning...all without facing the same challenges our spiritual forefathers faced.

Partly because our system of government was influenced by those fleeing persecution and sectarian violence. This gave us some protection against politically connected apostates coming against us for Godly living.

Our own lack of separation

But, another reason why Bible believing Christians today, including you and I, aren't facing the same level of persecution those 1st century NT Churches faced, or the various Christians throughout the last 2,000 years have faced, is because of our own lack of separation.

Because we compromise. Because we shirk standards and give in to the flesh, and aren't as perfect, or mature, as we could be. Worldly. Carnal.
